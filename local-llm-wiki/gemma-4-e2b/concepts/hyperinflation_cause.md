# Hyperinflation Cause

Hyperinflation is fundamentally understood within the corpus as being driven by monetary expansion, stemming from fiscal imbalances, and the subsequent collapse in the public's willingness to hold currency. This concept posits that the mechanism of money creation, coupled with shifts in public confidence, are the primary drivers of hyperinflationary episodes.

## How the argument evolved

The analysis of hyperinflation causation in the 'In Gold We Trust' reports evolved across the documented years, moving from a focus on fiscal mechanics to incorporating behavioral and monetary demand factors. In **2012**, the argument established a direct causal link between government fiscal policy and inflationary outcomes. The report asserted that "Hyperinflation was always caused by money printing due to exorbitant budget deficits." This perspective was strongly framed by the claim that "In fact there is no example in history where hyperinflation was not caused by money creation due to exorbitant budget deficits."

The understanding deepened in **2015** by expanding the scope beyond mere money printing to include the psychological and demand-side elements that accelerate hyperinflationary spirals. While the fiscal deficit remains central, the analysis incorporated the role of monetary demand collapse. Specifically, the report introduced the view that "Rothbard defines the desperate fight against deflation and the associated priming of the pump as the cause of most hyperinflation events." Furthermore, the report highlighted a crucial underestimation by mainstream analysis, noting that "The collapse in the demand to hold money is a crucial factor thereby, which is at best underestimated by the mainstream, but in most cases simply ignored." Thus, the concept evolved from a purely quantitative view of money creation to a more comprehensive model that integrates fiscal imbalance with the behavioral dynamics of currency demand.
