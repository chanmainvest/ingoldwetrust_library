# Gold as a Store of Value

Gold is fundamentally presented as a crucial asset for preserving purchasing power and acting as a reliable store of value, contrasting sharply with fiat currencies and paper-based instruments. This concept posits gold's role not just as a commodity, but as an essential anchor of trust and a hedge against systemic monetary instability across various economic cycles.

## How the argument evolved

The perspective on gold as a store of value has evolved across the analyzed reports, moving from a basic contrast with currency to a sophisticated view of its role in geopolitical and institutional finance. In **2015**, the initial framing established gold as a direct protector of assets against monetary instability. The argument was stark: "paper money couldn't possibly be regarded as a safe store of value," and gold "stands as a protector of property rights."

By **2021**, the narrative deepened, shifting the focus toward gold's function during periods of systemic stress. Gold was characterized as a long-term store of value and an anchor of trust, particularly when geopolitical tensions rise. The analysis highlighted its role as the "most reserve" of reserve assets, emphasizing that gold "diversifies the geopolitical risk and is a kind of anchor of trust, especially in times of tension and crises."

The treatment continued to evolve into a more forward-looking, institutional view by **2023**. Here, the focus was explicitly on the preservation of purchasing power against governmental actions. Gold was framed as a safe asset because "a gold piece, inaccessible to the inflationary policies of governments, would preserve its purchasing power better than a banknote."

Finally, in **2024**, the concept matured to encompass the role of gold within the global financial architecture. The analysis moved beyond individual protection to recognize gold's increasing centrality to major state actors. It is now viewed as "increasingly becoming the golden queen on the geo-economic chessboard," specifically noting that "for state actors such as central banks and sovereign wealth funds, gold is increasingly becoming the golden queen."
