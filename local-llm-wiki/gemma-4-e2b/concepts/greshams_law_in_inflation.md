# Gresham’s Law in Inflation

Gresham’s Law describes a phenomenon where the presence of two forms of money, one overvalued and one undervalued, leads to the displacement of the inferior currency by the superior one. This concept is crucial for understanding how inflation erodes the value of fiat currency and how monetary dynamics influence currency acceptance.

## How the argument evolved

The application of Gresham's Law within the corpus evolved across the analyzed reports, moving from an observation of currency decline in early inflationary periods to a more formal statement about the displacement of money based on relative valuation. In the **2012** report, the concept was introduced to explain the initial stages of inflation. The analysis noted that "Gresham’s Law applies in early inflation stages, leading to the decline of accepted currency." Specifically, the report illustrated that "if in case of a bimetal standard the central bank tries to defend a fixed parity," the mechanism results in "the acceptance of the currency falls abruptly due to the sped-up depreciation."

The understanding deepened in the **2020** analysis, which framed the concept in terms of relative monetary value. While retaining the core idea of displacement, the **2020** chapter provided a more precise definition of the relationship between overvalued and undervalued money. It stated that Gresham's law applies in inflation where "overvalued money displaces undervalued money, which is called Thiers' law." This is explicitly defined as: "Gresham’s law, according to which legally overvalued “bad” money displaces legally undervalued “good” money." The report further clarified the inverse relationship, noting that "This flight into “good” money is called Thiers’ law or Gresham’s law in reverse." Thus, the evolution shows a progression from describing the observable consequence of currency depreciation in early inflation to establishing a formal, reciprocal relationship between overvalued and undervalued money in the context of inflation dynamics.
