# Central Bank Quantitative Easing

Central Bank Quantitative Easing (QE) refers to the massive asset purchase programs undertaken by central banks, characterized by their enormous scale and indefinite duration. These programs represent a significant intervention into global financial markets, and the reports treat them as a defining feature of the modern monetary landscape, suggesting they carry profound implications for asset valuation.

## How the argument evolved

The initial framing of QE in the reports established the sheer magnitude of the undertaking. In **2013**, the focus was on the unprecedented nature of the commitment, noting that "The current QE program is the most massive put ever written." This perspective emphasized that "Both the extent of the current asset purchase program as well as its temporal horizon are unlimited," suggesting a sustained, long-term commitment from the central banks.

By **2016**, the discussion around QE had evolved to incorporate comparative metrics, attempting to put the scale of these monetary interventions into a broader context. The narrative shifted to comparing the volume of these purchases against tangible global benchmarks. For instance, the reports began to measure "The amount printed by the ECB and the BoJ" against global production figures, aiming to provide a "size of QE programs into perspective." This evolution suggests a move from simply documenting the commitment's size to attempting to contextualize it against real-world production capacities, such as when examining the "ECB's asset purchases in the first quarter."
