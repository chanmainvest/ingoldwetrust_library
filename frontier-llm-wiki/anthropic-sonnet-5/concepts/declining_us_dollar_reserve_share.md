# Declining US Dollar Reserve Share

The declining US dollar reserve share refers to the steady erosion of the greenback's dominance among central banks' foreign exchange reserves, a slow-moving but historically significant shift away from the currency that has anchored the post-war monetary order. In the In Gold We Trust reports, this trend is presented as a structural indicator of de-dollarization and a supporting pillar for the broader thesis of gold's remonetisation, since a weakening dollar reserve position implies central banks seeking alternative stores of value.

## How the argument evolved

**2021** introduced the theme by citing IMF data showing that "the share of US dollars in international currency reserves has recently fallen significantly to a level last seen in 1995." At that point, the dollar still commanded a substantial position, accounting for "around 59% of currency reserves," and the report framed this as evidence of a slow, ongoing diversification trend among central banks rather than a dramatic rupture. The narrative here was measured — the dollar remained the dominant reserve currency, but the direction of travel was already notable and worth tracking within the broader de-dollarization discussion alongside Europe's gold buying and China's digital currency ambitions.

By **2023**, the tone sharpened considerably. The report now framed the decline as accelerating rather than merely continuing, noting that "the US dollar's share as a global reserve currency fell at ten times the average pace of the past 20 years." This acceleration was explicitly linked to the geopolitical shock of the 2022 sanctions on Russia, which appear to have catalyzed reserve managers to reconsider dollar concentration risk. The report quantified the longer-term erosion starkly: the dollar's reserve share had "dropped to only 58% in 2022 – a far cry from its share of 73% in 2001," and had "lost about 11% of its market share since 2016." This framing — "The Final Showdown" — positioned the dollar's declining reserve status not as a slow drift but as part of a culminating confrontation in the international monetary system, with de-dollarization now presented as a structural and possibly irreversible force rather than a background statistic.

Taken together, the two chapters trace an arc from cautious observation to urgent emphasis: the 2021 note treats the dollar's fall to a 1995-era share as a noteworthy but incremental development, while the 2023 note recasts the same underlying trend as dramatically accelerated and geopolitically triggered, with concrete figures (73% to 58%, an 11% loss since 2016, tenfold pace increase) used to argue that the postwar dollar-centric reserve system is under serious and mounting pressure.
