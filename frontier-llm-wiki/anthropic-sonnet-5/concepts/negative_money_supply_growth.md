# Negative Money Supply Growth

Negative money supply growth refers to an outright contraction in the stock of money — most notably US M2 — rather than merely a slowdown in its rate of expansion. In the 'In Gold We Trust' corpus, this phenomenon is treated as a historically rare and ominous signal, one that has in the past coincided with banking crises, depressions, and deflationary episodes, and which in 2023 was read as a harbinger of disinflation, economic slump, and heightened financial fragility.

## How the argument evolved

**2023** marks the year this concept enters the report's analysis in force, introduced with striking historical framing. In the introductory chapter, the authors note that "money supply growth in the US, calculated on a monthly basis, is negative for the first time since the 1950s, and on an annual basis for the first time since the Great Depression." This is not presented as a neutral statistical curiosity but as a warning sign grounded in historical pattern-matching: "whenever US M2 money supply has contracted on an annual basis, there's been a banking crisis, a depression and/or deflation." By invoking the Great Depression and past banking crises, the report frames negative M2 growth as a rare regime shift rather than an ordinary business-cycle fluctuation, setting a tone of caution for the rest of the year's analysis.

This theme is then carried forward and connected explicitly to the inflation narrative in the chapter on the status quo of the inflation trend. There, the same historic milestone is restated — "In the USA, money supply growth is negative for the first time since the Great Depression" — but now situated within a broader constellation of recessionary indicators. The report links the contraction in money supply to other classic warning signs, noting that "the inverted yield curve and poor consumer sentiment are already strong signs of an economic slump." The logical chain being built is one where monetary contraction feeds into recession, and recession in turn feeds into disinflation, as captured in the report's blunt generalization: "Recessions are always disinflationary." In this way, negative money supply growth is woven directly into the year's central inflation debate — it becomes a key piece of evidence used to argue that the inflationary impulses of prior years were giving way to disinflationary or even deflationary risk, even as structural forces (analyzed elsewhere in the report) might reassert themselves over the longer term. Across both chapters, the concept functions as a bridge between monetary mechanics and macro forecasting: it substantiates claims about an impending economic slowdown while also serving as historical context that lends weight and urgency to the report's broader thesis about the fragility of the current monetary and financial system.
