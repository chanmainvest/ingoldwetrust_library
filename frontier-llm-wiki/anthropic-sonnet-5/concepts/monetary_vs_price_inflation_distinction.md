# Monetary vs Price Inflation Distinction

The 'In Gold We Trust' report repeatedly returns to a foundational Austrian School distinction: "inflation" in its original sense refers to *monetary inflation* — the expansion of the money supply — while the rising prices most people call "inflation" are properly termed *price inflation*, the downstream consequence of that monetary expansion. This distinction matters because it reframes the inflation debate away from consumer price indices alone and toward the underlying driver, the money supply, which affects not just consumer goods but asset prices as well — a point central to the report's long-running gold thesis.

## How the argument evolved

**2018** introduces the distinction plainly in the context of the "Inflation vs. Deflation" debate, noting that "inflation originally means 'monetary inflation', i.e. the expansion of the money supply, while price inflation (i.e., continuously rising prices) is its consequence." Crucially, the chapter extends the concept beyond consumer prices, stressing that "monetary inflation does not only affect consumer prices but also asset prices" — laying groundwork for later arguments about gold and other assets absorbing excess liquidity even when official CPI figures appear tame.

By **2025**, the report revisits this same conceptual bedrock but with a more urgent tone, framing it explicitly as an Austrian School principle under threat of neglect. It states that "representatives of the Austrian School of Economics attach great importance to the distinction between monetary inflation and price inflation," reintroducing the pairing of "the monetary underfeed – money supply inflation" with "the inevitable consequence of an excessive expansion of the money supply – price inflation." The 2025 chapter reinforces this with a direct invocation of monetarist orthodoxy, citing the view that "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon in the sense that it is and can be produced only by a more rapid increase in the quantity of money than in output." Read together, the two chapters show a consistent through-line: rather than revising the underlying theory, the report's 2025 treatment doubles down on the same conceptual framework introduced in 2018, now positioning it as a distinction that mainstream commentary increasingly overlooks — implicitly justifying the report's continued focus on money-supply dynamics, rather than headline CPI, as the true barometer of inflationary risk and the rationale for gold as a monetary asset.
