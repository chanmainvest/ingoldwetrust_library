# Petrodollar System

The Petrodollar System refers to the arrangement, dating to the mid-1970s, by which the United States and Saudi Arabia agreed that oil would be priced and traded exclusively in US dollars, with producer nations "recycling" their surplus dollars into US treasuries. This informal pact has functioned as a structural pillar of dollar hegemony, embedding the greenback at the center of global commodity trade and capital flows. Across the In Gold We Trust reports, the petrodollar system serves as the historical foundation for the broader narrative of de-dollarization and gold's potential remonetisation.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** introduces the concept in its foundational form, describing "an informal agreement the US and Saudi Arabia arrived at in the mid 1970s" that ensured oil, and by extension "all other important commodities," would be traded "only in US dollars." The report emphasizes the mechanical loop underpinning dollar dominance: oil producers "recycle" their petrodollars into US treasuries, a cycle that channels global energy revenues back into dollar-denominated assets and thereby sustains American financial primacy.

**2020** revisits this history with more explicit framing, calling the arrangement "the deal of the century" — the US-Saudi pact of the 1970s guaranteeing that "oil would only be traded in US dollars." By this point the report situates the petrodollar system not merely as background history but as the very foundation now under strain, positioning the chapter's argument that "the endgame has begun" for dollar-centric global finance. The petrodollar deal is treated as the load-bearing structure whose erosion would mark a genuine turning point in the international monetary order.

**2021** deepens the analysis by generalizing the significance of the petrodollar pact beyond a mere bilateral arrangement, describing how "Washington signed on to its infamous petrodollar pact with Riyadh in the 1970s" and asserting that "the oil trade has been of elemental importance to the world's monetary operating system." Crucially, this chapter sharpens the analytical lens: what matters is "not so much who buys oil or how much, but with what currency they buy it." This reframing shifts the focus from volumes and counterparties to the currency denomination of trade itself — the true mechanism by which the petrodollar system enforces dollar dominance. In this telling, any diversification away from dollar-invoiced oil trade — whether through Chinese digital currency initiatives or European gold accumulation — represents a direct challenge to the system's foundations, linking the petrodollar concept explicitly to the accelerating de-dollarization narrative that runs through the 2020 and 2021 reports.

Taken together, the three chapters trace an arc: from establishing the historical facts of the petrodollar arrangement (2017), to framing it as a foundational deal now reaching its "endgame" (2020), to generalizing its logic and identifying currency choice in oil trade as the decisive variable in the ongoing de-dollarization process (2021).
