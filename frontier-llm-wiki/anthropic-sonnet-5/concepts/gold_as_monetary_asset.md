# Gold as Monetary Asset

At the heart of the In Gold We Trust reports lies a foundational analytical choice: gold is treated not as an industrial commodity subject to ordinary supply-and-demand dynamics, but as a monetary asset whose behavior reflects the health of the global economic and monetary system. This framing shapes the entire methodology of the series, positioning gold as a barometer of currency stability, central bank policy, and macroeconomic confidence rather than a raw material to be priced like copper or oil. The consistency of this thesis across multiple editions underscores its role as the interpretive lens through which all subsequent analysis — inflation, real rates, currency depreciation — is filtered.

## How the argument evolved

The framing of gold as a monetary asset was established from the outset and has remained remarkably stable across editions, with the authors refining and deepening the rationale rather than changing the core stance.

In **2014**, the report opens with a direct methodological declaration: "we analyze gold primarily as a monetary asset and not as an industrial commodity." This sets the tone for the entire series, signaling that the analysis to follow would prioritize monetary and macroeconomic drivers over commodity-market fundamentals like mining supply or jewelry demand.

By **2015**, the same principle is restated almost verbatim — "we analyze gold primarily as a monetary asset and not as a commodity" — reinforcing continuity in the report's analytical identity. This edition adds a rhetorical flourish that elevates the claim beyond methodology into a broader worldview, asserting that "If you don't own gold, you know neither history nor economics." This line signals an evolution in tone: the monetary-asset framing is no longer just a technical choice but is bound up with a historical and educational argument about gold's role across centuries of economic experience.

In **2016**, the language shifts subtly but meaningfully. The report again affirms that gold is analyzed "primarily as a monetary asset and not as an industrial commodity," preserving the original formulation, but now explicitly connects this framing to gold's diagnostic function: gold "is mirroring the state of the global economy and monetary architecture." This marks a maturation of the argument — from a simple assertion of methodology in 2014, to a historically-charged claim in 2015, to a fully articulated theory in 2016 in which gold serves as a real-time indicator of systemic monetary health. The 2016 formulation ties the monetary-asset concept directly to the broader thesis of the report: that gold's price and behavior reveal stresses and imbalances within the global monetary architecture that other assets do not capture.

Across the three years, the throughline is clear: the reports consistently reject a commodity-centric view of gold, instead building a case that gold's significance lies in its monetary role. What changes is the depth of justification — from a bare statement of analytical intent, to a historical/educational appeal, to an explicit theoretical link between gold and the state of the global monetary system.
