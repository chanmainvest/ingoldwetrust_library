# Survey-Based Inflation Expectations (Michigan)

The University of Michigan's survey-based inflation expectations gauge how ordinary consumers perceive future price pressures, distinct from market-implied or model-based inflation forecasts. In the In Gold We Trust reports, this metric serves as a real-time barometer of public psychology around inflation, one that reacts swiftly to political shocks and geopolitical crises — even if, as later noted, it is an imperfect predictor of actual realized inflation.

## How the argument evolved

**2025** introduced the Michigan survey data as evidence of a marked shift in inflation psychology following the November 2024 US elections. The report highlighted that "short-term inflation expectations have risen sharply since the US elections in November 2024 and already stand at 6.5% as of April," framing this as a rapid repricing of consumer sentiment in the wake of political change. Alongside this, the report noted that longer-horizon expectations had also moved substantially, with long-term inflation expectations rising "by 1.4 percentage points or 50% to 4.4%" year-on-year — a striking increase that suggested the shift was not merely a short-lived reaction but potentially a more durable change in inflation psychology.

**2026** revisited and extended this narrative, confirming the post-election spike and adding a new catalyst: the outbreak of the Iran war. The report reiterated that "with Donald Trump's election victory, short-term inflation expectations shot up sharply," noting they "peaked at 6.6% in May 2025" — a figure closely tracking the elevated reading cited in the prior year's report. Crucially, the 2026 chapter documented a second distinct shock to consumer inflation psychology: "the outbreak of the Iran War has significantly pushed up short-term inflation expectations (median) from 3.4% to 4.7%." This demonstrated that the survey measure remained highly sensitive to geopolitical disruption even after the initial post-election surge had presumably cooled between the two peaks. Importantly, the 2026 treatment also introduced a note of methodological caution, observing that survey measures "poorly predict actual inflation" — a qualification that tempered the earlier chapter's implicit treatment of the survey as a reliable leading indicator. This evolution reflects a maturing analytical stance: from presenting the Michigan data as a straightforward signal of rising inflation risk in 2025, to treating it in 2026 as a valuable but imperfect gauge of shifting public sentiment, one whose spikes are best understood as reactions to specific shocks (elections, war) rather than as forecasts of where inflation is actually headed.

Together, the two chapters trace a consistent thread: consumer inflation expectations, as measured by the Michigan survey, are highly reactive to political and geopolitical events, prone to sharp short-term spikes, and increasingly viewed by the report's authors as a sentiment indicator rather than a dependable predictive tool.
