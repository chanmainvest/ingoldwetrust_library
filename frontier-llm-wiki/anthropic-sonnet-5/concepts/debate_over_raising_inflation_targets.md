# Debate Over Raising Inflation Targets

The "Debate Over Raising Inflation Targets" refers to a recurring policy discussion in which economists and central bankers question whether the conventional inflation target — long anchored at 2% — should be raised or effectively abandoned. For the *In Gold We Trust* report, this debate is significant because it exposes a structural temptation within monetary policy: using higher inflation as a tool to erode the real value of sovereign debt, rather than confronting deficits directly. The authors treat calls to raise inflation targets not as a technical adjustment but as a signal of deeper fiscal and monetary distress, with direct implications for gold as a store of value.

## How the argument evolved

**2015** marked the report's first sustained engagement with this theme, framed around the aftermath of the financial crisis and the mounting debt burdens facing sovereigns. The authors observed that "there are more and more voices arguing in favor of raising inflation targets as the only practicable solution to lowering debt ratios," noting that prominent policymakers had begun openly suggesting that "higher rates of price inflation should be targeted" as a deliberate strategy. Crucially, the report did not treat this as a neutral technocratic proposal — it characterized the approach as fundamentally uncontrollable, warning that "we believe this is akin to gambling." This early framing established the report's enduring skepticism: raising inflation targets was less a considered policy tool than a high-stakes bet with the real economy and savers' wealth as collateral.

By **2023**, the debate had matured and become more concrete, reflecting years of intervening monetary experimentation and the post-pandemic inflation surge. The report traced the intellectual lineage of the idea back to "the IMF's then chief economist Olivier Blanchard in 2016, when he called for the inflation target to be raised to 4%," showing that what had been a fringe murmur in 2015 had by then become a mainstream academic proposal with institutional backing. Yet the political and institutional response had hardened against it: the report noted that "Jerome Powell recently rejected these calls," signaling that central bank leadership was publicly resisting formal target increases even as inflationary pressures persisted. Despite this official resistance, the 2023 chapter suggested a more subtle capitulation was underway — the possibility that "central banks will soon abandon the 2% inflation target, at least *de facto*" — implying that even without a formal announcement, tolerance for higher inflation was quietly becoming policy in practice. This evolution — from open advocacy, to institutional pushback, to suspected tacit abandonment — traces a arc in which the report's original 2015 warning about "gambling" with inflation targets appeared increasingly prescient, as the gap between stated targets and actual policy tolerance widened.
