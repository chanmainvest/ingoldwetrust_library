# Wage-Price Spiral Dynamics

Wage-price spiral dynamics describe the self-reinforcing loop in which rising consumer prices push workers to demand higher wages, which in turn raises production costs and feeds back into further price increases. The In Gold We Trust reports treat this as a critical litmus test for whether 1970s-style structural inflation is returning, since a sustained spiral would validate persistent inflation as a macro regime rather than a transitory shock — with direct implications for gold as a monetary hedge.

## How the argument evolved

**2022** introduces the concept explicitly as a historical warning sign, noting that "a specter from the 1970s is now suddenly back: the price-wage spiral." The chapter interrogates the direction of causality between wages and prices, citing a Moody's study suggesting causality runs in only one direction — from consumer prices to wages, rather than the reverse. Crucially, the report ties the possibility of a genuine spiral to monetary policy, arguing that such a "second-round effect is only possible if the money supply has been or is being significantly expanded." This framing positions the wage-price spiral not as an autonomous labor-market phenomenon but as a symptom contingent on prior monetary expansion — an important nuance that tempers the alarmist 1970s comparison while still taking the risk seriously.

**2024** revisits the theme with a more structural, demographic lens, explicitly recalling that "we addressed the risk of a wage-price spiral" in earlier analysis. The argument shifts toward labor-market fundamentals: the report contends that "the bargaining power of employees is significantly greater than in previous decades," driven by demographic shifts and rising strike activity, which raises the probability that wage pressures could sustain rather than merely follow inflation. This marks an evolution from the 2022 focus on money-supply-driven second-round effects toward a broader structural story about labor scarcity and worker leverage. The chapter also introduces a geographic nuance absent from 2022: immigration's differing effects on wage growth in the US versus Europe, noting that "significant (illegal) immigration is likely to have a dampening effect on wage growth for unskilled workers." This complicates the spiral narrative by suggesting that labor-supply dynamics — not just monetary conditions or bargaining power — can either accelerate or dampen wage-driven inflation depending on regional migration patterns.

Taken together, the two chapters trace a shift in emphasis: 2022 grounds the wage-price spiral in monetary causality and historical caution, while 2024 layers on demographic and labor-market structural forces, along with immigration as a moderating variable. The throughline across both years is skepticism toward oversimplified spiral narratives, paired with a persistent concern that the ingredients for a 1970s-style dynamic — monetary expansion, tightening labor markets, and shifting bargaining power — remain present.
