# Triffin Dilemma

The Triffin Dilemma describes the structural conflict facing any nation whose currency serves as the world's reserve currency: it must run persistent current account deficits to supply the global economy with sufficient liquidity, yet doing so steadily erodes confidence in the currency's long-term stability. First articulated by economist Robert Triffin, the dilemma is central to the In Gold We Trust reports' explanation of why dollar-based monetary systems are inherently unstable — and why gold repeatedly re-emerges as the alternative anchor when confidence frays.

## How the argument evolved

**2016** introduced the dilemma in structural terms, framing it as a conflict between a reserve issuer's short-term domestic priorities and its long-term international responsibilities. The report noted that "a nation that issues a global reserve currency is subject to a conflict of interests between short term domestic and long term international goals," and tied currency stability directly to trade balances: "a currency is only stable if the issuer's current account is in balance," yet paradoxically "the issuing nation has to have a current account deficit" to satisfy global demand for its money. This chapter established the paradox in the abstract, as part of a broader discussion of global inflation trends in a dollar-centric world.

**2020** gave the concept its name and historical grounding, explicitly crediting Robert Triffin's warning that a reserve issuer's national interests would inevitably clash with the interests of the international community relying on that currency. The report used this framework to explain the collapse of Bretton Woods, noting that "there were already more US dollars in circulation abroad than were covered by US gold reserves," a mismatch that culminated in 1971 when "US President Richard Nixon simply suspended the gold convertibility of the US dollar." Here the Triffin Dilemma became a historical diagnostic tool — the mechanism that had already broken one dollar-gold system and, by implication, threatened to strain the fiat system that replaced it, as the report's "de-dollarization" thesis began to take shape.

**2024** revisited the dilemma with sharper, more distilled language, reducing it to its essential mechanism: "the reserve currency country must run, or at least does run, persistent current account deficits to provide the rest of the world with reserves." This chapter extended the argument into the present geopolitical moment, linking the dilemma to the vulnerability of nations dependent on dollar liquidity — "All these countries borrow, save, and trade in a currency they cannot print" — as a driver of the eastern push toward gold and away from dollar dependency. Across the three years, the treatment moves from a theoretical structural conflict (2016), to a named historical cause of monetary system collapse (2020), to a live justification for de-dollarization and renewed gold demand (2024).
