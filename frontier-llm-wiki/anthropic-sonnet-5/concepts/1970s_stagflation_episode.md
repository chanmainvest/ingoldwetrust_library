# 1970s Stagflation Episode

The 1970s Stagflation Episode refers to the period when developed economies, most notably the United States, experienced the historically unusual combination of rising inflation and economic recession — a pairing that gave rise to the term "stagflation" itself. Within the In Gold We Trust reports, this episode serves as the archetypal historical benchmark for understanding both how growth-and-inflation regimes can defy conventional expectations and how devastating such a regime can be for traditional financial assets, making it a recurring reference point for scenario analysis and long-term asset allocation.

## How the argument evolved

The concept first enters the corpus in **2014**, where it is introduced primarily as a definitional anchor within a broader discussion of future growth and inflation scenarios. The report traces the etymology of the term directly to the decade's macroeconomic experience, noting that "the term was coined during the economic crisis of the 1970s, when the US went through a period of rising inflation rates in concert with an economic recession." At this stage, the episode functions mainly as historical context — establishing stagflation as a recognizable, named phenomenon that frames later scenario-building about how growth and inflation might interact in the future.

By **2016**, the treatment deepens considerably, shifting from definition to consequence. In the chapter on inflation and investment, the report revisits the 1970s not merely to explain terminology but to draw out its severe implications for asset markets. It observes that "only very few active investors still remember" how devastating that inflationary, stagflationary stretch actually was for developed nations — a pointed remark suggesting that market memory of the period had faded even as its lessons remained relevant. Crucially, the 2016 discussion quantifies the damage: the stagflationary years from 1966 to 1981 produced "the worst performance of stocks and bonds in 80 years," a stretch immediately followed by "the best two decades these asset classes ever experienced." This before-and-after framing transforms the 1970s episode from a mere historical curiosity into a cautionary case study about regime change — illustrating how a prolonged period of stagflation can crush conventional portfolios before giving way to a dramatic recovery, and implicitly warning investors not to assume that recent decades of benign inflation and strong equity/bond returns represent a permanent state of affairs.

Taken together, the two chapters trace a clear arc: the 2014 report lays the conceptual groundwork by naming and defining stagflation through its 1970s origins, while the 2016 report builds on that foundation to draw a sharper investment lesson — that stagflationary regimes, though rare and easily forgotten, can inflict multi-decade damage on stocks and bonds, making historical awareness of the 1970s essential for judging present-day risks to traditional asset classes.
