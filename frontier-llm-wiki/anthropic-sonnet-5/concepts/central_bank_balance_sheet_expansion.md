# Central Bank Balance Sheet Expansion

Central bank balance sheet expansion refers to the dramatic growth in the assets and liabilities held by major central banks—chiefly through base money creation—since the onset of the 2007 financial crisis. In the *In Gold We Trust* reports, this expansion is treated as a central driver of monetary inflation, a key reason for eroding confidence in fiat currencies and government debt, and a structural tailwind for gold as an alternative store of value.

## How the argument evolved

The theme first took shape in **2011**, when the report documented the scale of post-crisis intervention: "The ECB, the Federal Reserve, and the Bank of England have expanded their balance sheets by more than USD 4.5 trillion since 2007," alongside a claim that "the monetary base in the USA has increased by more than 30%." This expansion was directly linked to a loss of trust in traditional safe havens, as the report noted that "government bonds had to part with their 'risk-free' status last year"—framing balance sheet growth not as a technical policy detail but as a symptom of deeper systemic fragility in fiat money and sovereign debt.

By **2012**, the analysis became more quantitative and structural. The report calculated that "the combined base money supply of the four most important central banks has been growing by 15.2% per year since 2000," and tracked the absolute scale of this growth, noting that balance sheets "were growing from USD 3,500bn to almost USD 9,000bn." Crucially, the 2012 chapter introduced a warning about the open-ended nature of this policy tool, observing that "there's no limit to central bank expanding its balance sheet in theory"—a statement that shifted the narrative from documenting past expansion to flagging the absence of any natural ceiling on future monetary inflation.

By **2016**, the story had escalated from a broad-based, multi-central-bank phenomenon to one dominated by a single, extreme outlier: the Bank of Japan. The report described the BoJ as having "taken this insanity several steps further than their peers have managed," signaling that what began as coordinated post-crisis stimulus among Western central banks had evolved into increasingly aggressive and divergent experimentation. The scale was framed as historically unprecedented, with the BoJ's "leverage ratio and the size of its balance sheet relative to GDP" reaching levels never seen before. This marked a maturation of the argument: no longer just tallying trillions in expansion, the report now emphasized relative scale (balance sheet to GDP) and leverage as the more alarming metrics, and positioned central bank policy experimentation as an escalating, rather than stabilizing, force.

Across the five-year arc from 2011 to 2016, the treatment of balance sheet expansion thus moved from crisis-response documentation, to quantified structural critique with an explicit warning about limitless expansion, to a portrait of policy extremism exemplified by Japan—each stage reinforcing the report's broader thesis that unconstrained monetary expansion undermines fiat currency credibility and strengthens the case for gold.
