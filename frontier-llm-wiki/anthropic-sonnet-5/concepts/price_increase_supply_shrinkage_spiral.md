# Price-Increase Supply-Shrinkage Spiral

The Price-Increase Supply-Shrinkage Spiral describes a self-reinforcing inflationary mechanism in which rising costs — whether from energy, inputs, or borrowing — push producers to curtail output, and the resulting contraction in supply pushes prices higher still. Rather than inflation being cured by classic tightening tools, the spiral suggests that under certain conditions the very medicine (higher rates, higher costs) can worsen the disease by squeezing supply. This concept matters because it challenges the assumption that monetary tightening alone reliably tames inflation, and it underpins the *In Gold We Trust* report's broader case for structurally persistent, hard-to-control inflation.

## How the argument evolved

The concept was introduced in **2022**, directly tied to the energy crisis and surging input costs of that period. The report observed that "the (sharp) rise in production costs forces companies out of the market, causing supply to shrink, which further fuels the upward pressure on prices," explicitly naming this dynamic "a price-increase supply-shrinkage spiral." The evidence at this stage was concrete and immediate: "high energy prices and the extreme volatility of prices have already prompted some companies to limit their production," framing the spiral as a real-time consequence of the commodity and energy shock rather than a theoretical construct.

By **2023**, the report extended the mechanism beyond energy costs to the realm of monetary policy itself, arguing that "higher interest rates can have an inflationary effect in the short term." The logic shifted to financing costs: "higher borrowing costs lead to higher production costs for the same quantity of goods," meaning that central banks' own rate hikes could feed the very "price-increase supply-shrinkage spiral" they were meant to combat. This reframing was significant — it turned the spiral from a supply-side energy story into a critique of interest-rate policy, suggesting that tightening cycles carry an inherent risk of perpetuating rather than resolving inflation by raising the cost of doing business and constraining production.

Across the two years, the throughline is consistent: whatever the initial cost shock — energy prices in 2022, borrowing costs in 2023 — the transmission mechanism is the same. Elevated costs squeeze producer margins, output is curtailed, and the resulting supply shrinkage sustains or accelerates price increases, closing the loop and giving the spiral its self-reinforcing character.
