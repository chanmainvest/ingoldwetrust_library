# Central Bank Gold Buying Surge

The "Central Bank Gold Buying Surge" refers to the historic wave of official-sector gold accumulation that began in earnest in 2022, when central banks purchased gold at the fastest pace since record-keeping started in 1950. In the In Gold We Trust reports, this trend is treated as a structural pillar of the gold bull case — evidence that sovereign institutions are reasserting gold's monetary role — and is repeatedly compared to historic precedents to gauge how long and how powerful the resulting bull market for gold might become.

## How the argument evolved

**2023** established the empirical foundation for the thesis. The report highlighted that 2022 was a watershed year: "2022 saw the highest gold buying by central banks on record, i.e. since 1950, when the WGC started its records." Central banks collectively added 1,136 tonnes to their reserves, with Türkiye standing out as the largest single buyer, adding 148 tonnes. Crucially, the analysis went beyond the headline figures to note that official reporting likely understated the true scale of accumulation — the World Gold Council flagged "substantial" unreported purchases from institutions, amounting to roughly half of all buying. This framing positioned central bank demand not merely as a data point within the broader inflation narrative, but as a signal that sovereign actors were quietly and deliberately building gold reserves outside the transparency of standard reporting channels.

**2024** deepened the argument by placing the ongoing buying spree into long-run historical context. Rather than simply reiterating the record-breaking tonnage, the report drew a direct parallel to a pivotal earlier era: central banks were now buying gold "at more than 1,000 tonnes per year," a rate faster than that seen in the late 1960s — the period immediately preceding the collapse of the London Gold Pool in 1968. This historical anchor was significant because that earlier episode "acted as the catalyst for a gold bull market that lasted more than a decade." By invoking Roy Jastram's framework on gold's role through inflationary and deflationary cycles, the report used this comparison to pose a pointed, forward-looking question: "Is another gold bull market of similar length in prospect?" The argument thus evolved from documenting a statistical anomaly in 2023 to using that anomaly, in 2024, as the basis for a historically grounded thesis about the potential duration and magnitude of a new secular gold bull market — with central bank buying serving as both the trigger and the ongoing fuel.
