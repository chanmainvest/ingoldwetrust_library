# Modern Monetary Theory (MMT)

Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) is a macroeconomic framework, embraced increasingly by segments of the political left, that provides a theoretical justification for combining easy fiscal policy with easy monetary policy, treating government deficits and central bank accommodation as mutually reinforcing tools rather than constraints. Within the In Gold We Trust reports, MMT is significant as a marker of a broader ideological and policy paradigm shift — one that erodes traditional guardrails around monetary discipline and thereby strengthens the long-term case for gold as a hedge against currency debasement.

## How the argument evolved

**2019** introduces MMT as an emerging political phenomenon rather than a settled economic theory, noting that the Democratic left was "increasingly flirting with questionable monetary experiments that usually start with the buzzword MMT." At this early stage, the report frames MMT skeptically, characterizing it as essentially a rationalization device: "MMT is the post hoc justification of both easy fiscal policy and easy monetary policy." The concept is presented as a symptom of a political environment growing comfortable with unconstrained spending and money creation.

By **2020**, MMT has moved from the political fringe toward the center of policy discourse, propelled by the fiscal emergency of the pandemic. The report reflects on its own prior year's treatment, recalling that "last year, we dealt extensively with Modern Monetary Theory (MMT), the inflationists' new favorite" — signaling that MMT is now understood as a key driver of inflationist expectations rather than a mere political curiosity. Crucially, the 2020 chapter argues that the conditions favoring an actual shift toward MMT-style policy had intensified: "the likelihood of such an economic and monetary policy paradigm shift has increased noticeably," tying MMT directly to the unprecedented fiscal deficits generated by pandemic-era spending. The report also deepens the historical grounding of the concept, pointing to a precedent from 1930s Japan to argue that MMT is not truly novel: "the following anecdote from 1930s Japan proves that MMT is by no means a 'modern' theory." This historical framing situates MMT within a recurring pattern of monetary experimentation during periods of fiscal stress, reinforcing the report's broader inflation-dynamics narrative — that the more governments lean on MMT-style reasoning, the more likely a lasting inflationary paradigm shift becomes.
