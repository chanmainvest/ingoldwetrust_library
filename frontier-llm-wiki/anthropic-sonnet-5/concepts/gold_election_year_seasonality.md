# Gold Election-Year Seasonality

Gold Election-Year Seasonality refers to the recurring pattern in gold's price behavior tied to the four-year US presidential election cycle, in which the metal tends to post subdued or choppy returns during election years themselves, followed by markedly stronger performance in the year after. This pattern matters to technical analysts within the report because it offers a calendar-based framework for anticipating turning points and managing expectations around election-year volatility, independent of fundamental drivers.

## How the argument evolved

The concept was first introduced in **2012**, where the report's technical analysis chapter identified a clear historical asymmetry: gold "often ends up on the negative side in election years," with average annual performance of just 1.3%, a figure well below gold's typical long-run returns. Crucially, the 2012 analysis paired this observation with a bullish counterpoint — the year *following* a US election has historically been far more rewarding for gold, with "the average performance of those years" reaching 11.9%. This early framing established the core election-year thesis: expect relative weakness during the election year, followed by strength once the political cycle resolves.

By **2020**, the report refined this seasonality argument with greater intra-year granularity, moving beyond annual averages to map out specific seasonal windows within election years themselves. Rather than treating the election year as uniformly weak, the 2020 technical analysis chapter described a more textured pattern of shifting tailwinds and reversals. It noted that "seasonal tailwinds set in from mid-May but are reversed at the beginning of July," pinpointing a short-lived bullish phase in late spring that fades as summer begins. The chapter then identified a second seasonal window later in the year, observing that "another seasonally strong phase begins in mid-September," which in ordinary years might continue for some time — but in election years specifically, "this ends abruptly at the beginning of October." This addition sharpened the concept considerably: the disruption to normal seasonal strength is explicitly tied to the electoral calendar, suggesting that political uncertainty in the run-up to the vote curtails what would otherwise be a favorable autumn period for gold.

Taken together, the two chapters show the argument evolving from a broad, annual-average observation in 2012 into a more granular, month-by-month seasonal map in 2020. The throughline across both years is the idea that US elections introduce a distinct disruption to gold's normal price rhythm — dampening returns and truncating seasonal strength during the election year — while the 2012 chapter's forward-looking observation about the strong post-election year remains an implicit backdrop against which the 2020 chapter's within-year seasonal detail can be read. Together, the notes suggest a maturing analytical approach: from identifying that election years are weak, to explaining precisely when and how that weakness manifests within the calendar.
