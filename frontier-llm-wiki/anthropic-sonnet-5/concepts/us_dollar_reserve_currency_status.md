# US Dollar Reserve Currency Status

The US dollar's position as the world's dominant reserve currency has underpinned American financial and geopolitical power for decades, but the In Gold We Trust reports track mounting evidence that this status is eroding. As central banks diversify reserves and structural US vulnerabilities—chronic deficits, debt, and dependence on external financing—come into sharper focus, the reports frame de-dollarization as a slow-moving but significant trend with direct implications for gold as an alternative store of value.

## How the argument evolved

**2019** introduced the theme of de-dollarization as a phenomenon spreading beyond traditional skeptics to Europe itself, hence the chapter's framing that "Europe Joins The Party." The report highlighted concrete data showing the dollar's declining share of international reserves, noting it had "fell to its lowest level for years" at 63%. This was paired with structural warnings about US fiscal fragility, citing that "forty percent of the U.S. deficit is funded by external factors," a dependency the report stressed no other country shares. Adding weight to the argument, the piece invoked a prominent voice from within the financial establishment—BlackRock's Larry Fink—who conceded that "the US dollar's status as the world's reserve currency will not be sustainable forever." Together, these threads positioned dollar hegemony not as an immutable fact but as a condition contingent on debt, deficits, and geopolitical tension.

By **2020**, the argument sharpened and escalated, as reflected in the chapter title declaring "The Endgame Has Begun." The report broadened the historical lens, pointing to IMF data showing the dollar's share of foreign reserves falling from a peak of 73% in 2001 to 62% by the end of the prior year—a multi-decade decline rather than a recent blip. This longer time horizon lent the narrative a sense of inevitability, encapsulated in the stark assertion that "the fate of reserve currencies is to decline over time." The language also grew more pointed regarding what was at stake: not merely a diminished share of reserves, but the possibility that the dollar could be "at risk of surrendering its lead, if not its role, as the world's preeminent reserve currency." This marked a shift from questioning sustainability (2019) to actively framing 2020 as the beginning of an "endgame"—a turning point rather than a distant possibility.

Across both years, the reports built a cumulative case: from raising doubts about the dollar's future in 2019 to asserting that the historical process of reserve-currency decline was already underway in 2020. The consistent use of hard reserve-share statistics alongside rhetorical framing ("party," "endgame") reflects the reports' broader thesis that monetary history moves in cycles, and that the dollar's turn to relinquish its throne may be approaching—a dynamic the In Gold We Trust reports consistently link to gold's re-emergence as a monetary anchor.
