# Bretton Woods Gold Exchange Standard

The Bretton Woods Gold Exchange Standard was the post-World War II monetary architecture that pegged the US dollar to gold at USD 35 an ounce while all other currencies were pegged to the dollar, making the greenback the world's reserve anchor. Within the "In Gold We Trust" corpus, the system serves as the historical template for understanding today's dollar-based order and, crucially, for anticipating how such a system tends to unravel when its internal contradictions become unsustainable.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** introduces the Bretton Woods system primarily as a cautionary historical parallel within the broader de-dollarization narrative. The report notes approvingly that a commentator "praises the Bretton Woods gold exchange standard, which tied the dollar and all other currencies to gold," using this as a benchmark of monetary discipline against which the current fiat-dollar regime is found wanting. More pointedly, the chapter warns that history could rhyme: "the end result could be very similar to the Bretton Woods end game," raising the possibility that "the US president could eventually be forced to close the gold window again" — an explicit echo of Nixon's 1971 suspension of dollar-gold convertibility. Here the concept functions less as a technical explainer and more as a warning shot, framing the current dollar system as vulnerable to the same kind of collapse.

**2020**, in the chapter "De-Dollarization 2020: The Endgame Has Begun," returns to Bretton Woods with a fuller structural account of how and why the system was built — and why it was destined to strain. The report explains that "the Americans did not care. They saw only the advantages and introduced the Bretton Woods gold exchange standard," underscoring that the US embraced the arrangement chiefly for the privileges it conferred on the dollar as reserve currency. It details the mechanics: the US was bound to "convert their currency into gold on behalf of foreign central banks and governments at USD 35 an ounce," while the flip side of dollar hegemony was that "the USA had to run a permanent trade deficit" — a condition the report stresses "is still true today." This later treatment thus deepens the 2017 framing: rather than merely gesturing at a possible repeat collapse, it traces the structural logic — reserve-currency privilege paired with chronic deficits — that made the original Bretton Woods system's demise inevitable, and it uses that logic to explain the persistent imbalances still present in the dollar system decades after gold convertibility was severed. Together, the two chapters move from an implicit historical warning to an explicit structural diagnosis, reinforcing the report's broader thesis that dollar-centric monetary orders sow the seeds of their own "endgame."
