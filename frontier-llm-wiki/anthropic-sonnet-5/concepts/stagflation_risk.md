# Stagflation Risk

Stagflation Risk refers to the danger of an economy experiencing the toxic combination of stagnant or below-potential growth alongside persistent or rising inflation — a scenario that defies conventional policy responses and erodes real returns across most asset classes. In the In Gold We Trust reports, this concept recurs as a recurring warning signal, evolving from an early diagnosis of incipient symptoms into a more confident structural forecast, making it central to the reports' broader inflation-versus-deflation debate and their case for gold.

## How the argument evolved

**2014** marks the origin of the theme, with the report identifying the "beginnings of a stagflation period" as a direct consequence of prolonged negative real interest rates and looming supply shocks. The authors frame this as an inevitable outcome of loose monetary policy, memorably describing stagflation as "the legacy of the Bernanke regime." At this stage the mechanism is explained in classic terms: stagflation is "generally triggered by supply shocks (price shocks)," which push up the general price level through rising cost pressures even as growth remains weak.

By **2017**, the argument sharpens into a concrete diagnosis of the present moment rather than a future prediction. The report points to hard data — US GDP expanding by only "a meagre 1.2% y/y" in Q1 2017 — paired with inflation running "more than 2%," and concludes that the US economy is "at the edge of stagflation." This represents a shift from the 2014 framing of early warning signs to an assessment that the conditions have now materialized in measurable form, tying the stagflation thesis directly to contemporaneous economic indicators rather than structural monetary critique alone.

By **2020**, the tone hardens further into a probabilistic and structural forecast. The report explicitly states that "the probability of a stagflationary scenario has increased," defining it precisely as the coincidence of "economic stagnation, i.e. low, below-potential growth and noticeable inflation." Crucially, the horizon extends beyond a cyclical episode: the authors suggest markets "at some point be facing a pronounced phase of stagflation in the decade ahead," reframing stagflation not as a passing risk but as a defining feature of the coming economic era. This represents the culmination of the argument's arc — from identifying nascent symptoms in 2014, to confirming edge-of-stagflation conditions in 2017, to forecasting a durable, decade-long stagflationary regime in 2020.

Across all three chapters, the throughline is consistent: weak growth and elevated inflation are treated not as separate problems but as a linked pathology rooted in monetary policy excess and real economic constraints (supply shocks, below-potential growth). Each installment builds on the last, moving from theoretical mechanism, to empirical confirmation, to long-term structural prognosis — collectively reinforcing the reports' broader case that gold serves as a hedge against exactly this kind of dual threat to both growth and price stability.
