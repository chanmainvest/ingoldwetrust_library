# Renminbi Internationalization

Renminbi internationalization refers to China's sustained, multi-front campaign to elevate its currency from a domestic instrument into a global unit of trade invoicing, reserve holding, and settlement — a project the In Gold We Trust reports treat as a structural pillar of broader de-dollarization. Because a credible renminbi alternative would erode the US dollar's exorbitant privilege, the reports track this evolution as a slow-moving but consequential force reshaping the international monetary order, one intertwined at points with gold itself.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** introduces the theme through the lens of gold and official recognition. China is described as "making a great effort to establish the renminbi as an international currency," with the report highlighting the milestone of the renminbi's inclusion "in the currency basket of the IMF's special drawing rights" as evidence of growing institutional legitimacy. Notably, this early treatment links the currency's ambitions directly to the gold market, suggesting "the renminbi has the potential to become the invoicing currency for gold" — framing internationalization not just as a monetary policy goal but as part of a broader challenge to dollar-based commodity pricing.

**2021** shifts to a more sober, structural assessment. The report emphasizes that rhetoric must be backed by financial infrastructure: "to establish the renminbi as a long-term alternative to the US dollar, Beijing needs to create large and liquid bond markets," and notes that "the central bank wants to move more towards liberalising its markets" and securing inclusion in global bond indices. Yet the report tempers expectations with a stark reserve-currency statistic — "only 2% of global foreign reserves are held in renminbi, while about 60% are held in US dollars and 20% in euros" — underscoring that despite policy effort, the renminbi's actual footprint in global reserves remained marginal four years after its SDR inclusion.

**2024** returns to the theme with renewed urgency, situating it within an intensifying "Eastern Push for Gold" and broader de-dollarization narrative. China is again described as "attempting to raise the renminbi's status as a potential global reserve currency," now pursued through swap lines, LNG trade deals, and expanding payment infrastructure. The reserve-share figure has crept up only modestly — "the renminbi represents only 2.5% of FX reserves" — showing that seven years of effort have produced incremental, not transformative, gains on that metric. However, a more dynamic indicator emerges: the renminbi "achieved a record-high share of 4.6% in global payments by value," suggesting that transactional usage is advancing faster than reserve accumulation. Read together, the three chapters trace a consistent arc: ambitious institutional signaling (2017) giving way to recognition of the hard infrastructural work required (2021), culminating in tangible but still modest progress on both reserve status and payments, layered onto an increasingly assertive geopolitical de-dollarization push (2024).
