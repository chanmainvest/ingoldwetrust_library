# Monetary Tectonics

"Monetary Tectonics" is the In Gold We Trust reports' metaphor for the persistent, unstable struggle between disinflationary/deflationary market forces and politically induced inflationary pressures within the monetary system. Like tectonic plates grinding against one another, these opposing forces build tension that occasionally releases in sudden, disruptive shifts — a dynamic the reports treat as essential for investors to understand when positioning around gold.

## How the argument evolved

The concept was introduced in **2015**, framing the global monetary system as the site of an ongoing conflict between disinflationary and inflationary forces. The report stated plainly that "this conflict, which we have dubbed 'monetary tectonics', is very important for investors," and diagnosed the prevailing regime at the time: "disinflationary forces have clearly had the upper hand since 2011." This dominance of disinflation was illustrated starkly through the commodity complex, where the simultaneous collapse in prices across commodities was described in seismic terms as "this concurrent devaluation of all commodities is a disinflationary earthquake" — extending the geological metaphor to characterize a sudden, violent release of built-up pressure rather than a gradual shift.

By **2018**, the framework had matured into a recurring analytical lens rather than a one-off observation, with the report noting that it had "often referred to the interdependencies of inflationary and deflationary forces as 'monetary tectonics.'" The emphasis shifted from documenting which side held the upper hand toward stressing the underlying instability of the system itself: "the risk of a deflationary shock is always around the corner." The 2018 treatment reframed the conflict explicitly as a contest between organic market forces and deliberate policy intervention, observing that "the test of strength between a market-cleansing bout of deflation and the inflation induced by politics continued vigorously." This reveals an evolution in the argument's texture — from a 2015 snapshot of disinflation's temporary victory to a 2018 recognition that the tension itself is chronic and structural, with politics actively working to counteract market-driven deflationary cleansing, and with the possibility of sudden rupture never fully off the table.

Across both chapters, the throughline is consistent: monetary tectonics is not a prediction of which force wins, but a diagnostic tool for understanding why the monetary system oscillates unpredictably between inflationary and deflationary regimes, and why investors — particularly those positioned in gold — must stay alert to the risk of abrupt, earthquake-like shifts even during periods when one force appears dominant.
