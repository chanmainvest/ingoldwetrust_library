# Gold Seasonality Patterns

Gold Seasonality Patterns refer to the recurring, calendar-based tendencies in gold's price behavior — periods of the year that historically favor strength versus stretches of stagnation or weakness. Across the In Gold We Trust reports' technical analysis chapters, this concept has served as a recurring tool for timing accumulation and interpreting short-term price action against a longer-term bullish backdrop. Its significance lies in helping investors distinguish between a bear-market correction and a normal, historically-grounded seasonal lull.

## How the argument evolved

The seasonality thesis first appears in **2014**, framed simply and practically: gold exhibits recurring seasonal price patterns, with mid-year offering an attractive entry point and the latter part of the year showing the greatest strength. The report states plainly that "June is traditionally a good time to buy" and identifies "September is traditionally the strongest month," concluding that "in the second half of the year upward momentum is clearly at its highest."

**2015** reinforces this same framework almost verbatim, reaffirming that "gold exhibits clear seasonal trends" and that "traditionally, June is an excellent time for purchases," with "seasonal upside momentum... clearly at its strongest in the second half of the year." At this stage the argument remains a general seasonal heuristic tied to the bottoming-phase narrative of those years, used to bolster the case that a multi-year low was near.

By **2017**, the treatment becomes far more quantitative and precise. Rather than a loose seasonal window, the report now pinpoints that "gold prices typically rise from early August to the end of February," attributing "the vast bulk of gold's 7.5% average annual price gain" to this specific stretch. It sharpens the contrast further by noting that the remaining 167 days of the year — from late February to early August — generated "a negligible 0.30%" return, turning seasonality into a near-mechanical calendar model of gold's annual performance.

**2018** refines the timing slightly, shifting the start of the favorable window earlier: "Gold typically rises from the beginning of July to the end of February." It also introduces a new layer of nuance by linking seasonality to the political cycle, observing that "in years of US midterm elections... the seasonality is even more profound," suggesting that macro-political timing can amplify the underlying seasonal pattern.

By **2026**, the seasonality discussion becomes more observational and adaptive rather than prescriptive. The report notes that "the high for this year was reached unusually early," acknowledging that actual price behavior can deviate from the historical calendar template. It reaffirms the long-standing view that "the precious-metals sector is statistically in a less favorable phase during spring," and uses this framework to set expectations for a possible "breathing space lasting several weeks to several months" rather than a definitive top or bottom — showing seasonality's evolution from a buy-signal tool into a broader interpretive lens for market pauses.
