# Gold/Oil Ratio

The Gold/Oil Ratio measures how many barrels of crude oil one ounce of gold can purchase, offering a cross-commodity lens on relative value and economic conditions. Because gold tends to behave as a monetary and crisis asset while oil is tightly linked to industrial demand and growth, shifts in the ratio serve as a barometer of economic cycles, currency depreciation, and the relative strength of "real money" versus energy. The report treats the ratio less as a trading signal and more as a historical yardstick, comparing current readings against long-term medians to judge whether gold is cheap or dear relative to oil.

## How the argument evolved

**2009** introduced the ratio explicitly as a cyclical and monetary indicator, noting that "an increasing gold/oil ratio tends to go hand in hand with recessions and economic turbulences," while "a falling ratio tends to indicate economic prosperity and stability." At the time, the ratio stood at 16, described as "only slightly above its historical median of 15" — framing gold as essentially fairly valued against oil and implicitly signaling that the economy sat close to a historical equilibrium rather than deep in crisis or boom.

By **2012**, the framing shifted slightly toward a longer historical narrative of relative performance between the two assets. The report noted that "one ounce of gold currently buys 15 barrels of oil," again landing almost exactly on the long-term median identified three years earlier. But the more significant addition was the historical context: the analysis observed that "almost 25 years of outperformance of oil are coming to an end, and gold is gaining in relative strength again." This reframed the ratio not just as a snapshot of fair value, but as a turning point in a multi-decade cycle, with gold poised to reassert strength after a long stretch in which oil had outperformed it.

Taken together, the two years show a consistent anchor point — a ratio near 15–16 as the historical median — but an evolving interpretation of what sits on either side of that median. In 2009 the ratio was used to characterize the *current* economic mood (turbulence vs. stability), while in 2012 it was used to characterize a *structural* shift in the balance of power between gold and oil, suggesting the years-long tailwind for oil was fading and gold's relative strength was reasserting itself.
