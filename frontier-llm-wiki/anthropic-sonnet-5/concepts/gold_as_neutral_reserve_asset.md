# Gold as Neutral Reserve Asset

Gold's enduring appeal as a reserve holding rests on a single structural fact: it is nobody's liability. Unlike the US dollar or any fiat currency, gold carries no counterparty risk, is not subject to the political decisions of any one government, and has been accumulated by central banks across ideological and geopolitical divides. For the *In Gold We Trust* report, this "neutrality" is not incidental—it is the very quality that positions gold as a potential anchor for a post-dollar international monetary order, and the concept recurs and deepens across the years as de-dollarization accelerates.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** lays the conceptual groundwork, introducing gold explicitly as "a neutral reserve asset held by more than a hundred countries in their central banks," and stressing that this makes it "nonpolitical" in a way no national currency can be. The report goes further, speculating about gold's suitability as the foundation of a new global monetary standard, noting it is "hard to imagine that it could be any other standard than gold."

**2019** builds on this by framing gold as not merely neutral but crisis-resistant, calling it "the only truly neutral asset available to governments and central banks." The chapter emphasizes gold's psychological and institutional function—"a bar of gold retains its value, even in times of crisis"—and crystallizes the idea into a memorable formulation: gold as "the perfect piggy bank" and "the anchor of trust for the financial system." Here neutrality is tied explicitly to trust-building among central banks, especially as European institutions join the accumulation trend.

**2020** shifts the argument from theory to observable behavior: central banks' actual purchasing patterns become evidence of gold's rising status. The report notes that "central banks are sounding by not buying Treasuries but buying gold on a net basis over the last six years," treating this shift as a real-world signal that gold is displacing sovereign debt as the preferred reserve vehicle. The discussion moves toward mechanics of a possible transition, floating ideas such as using "gold as a reference point for inflation expectations for all SDR currencies" and even "a one-time devaluation of all currencies against gold"—suggesting gold's neutrality could be operationalized within a restructured, gold-referenced currency system.

**2024** delivers the most systemic articulation of the thesis, connecting gold's neutrality directly to the structural flaws of the dollar-based order. The report argues that "gold serving as a neutral reserve asset doesn't just fix Triffin's Dilemma, it obviates it completely," positioning gold not as a supplementary hedge but as a direct replacement for US Treasuries: "today, gold can still replace USTs as the de facto reserve asset." The chapter closes the arc opened in 2017 with a slogan capturing the culmination of the thesis: the old "TINA doctrine would now be replaced by TIAA – There Is An Alternative – and the alternative is gold."

Across seven years, the narrative progresses from an abstract observation about gold's political neutrality, through its emotional and institutional framing as an "anchor of trust," to empirical evidence of central bank behavior, and finally to a structural argument that gold could resolve the foundational imbalances of the dollar-reserve system itself.
