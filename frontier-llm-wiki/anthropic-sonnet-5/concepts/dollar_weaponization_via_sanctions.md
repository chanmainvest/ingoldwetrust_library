# Dollar Weaponization via Sanctions

The concept of "Dollar Weaponization via Sanctions" describes how the United States leverages the dollar's central role in global finance — and its control over infrastructure like SWIFT — to enforce foreign policy objectives through sanctions, secondary sanctions, and asset freezes. In the In Gold We Trust reports, this weaponization is treated as a key structural driver of de-dollarization: by turning the dollar-based system into a coercive tool, the US incentivizes other nations to seek alternatives, ultimately undermining the very dominance it seeks to exploit and strengthening the long-term case for gold as a neutral reserve asset.

## How the argument evolved

**2019** introduced the theme squarely within the Trump administration's foreign policy: "The Trump administration is increasingly using the dollar as a weapon to pursue foreign policy goals." The report pointed to concrete enforcement actions — Iran's expulsion from SWIFT ("he has managed to get SWIFT to expel Iran again") and the heavy fines imposed on European banks such as BNP Paribas and Commerzbank for violating US sanctions — as evidence that dollar dominance was being actively converted into a geopolitical lever, with European institutions bearing collateral damage despite not being the primary sanctions target.

**2020** deepened this narrative by generalizing the mechanism beyond individual cases: the US had "set unilateral rules on secondary sanctions that abuse its global financial dominance," meaning that virtually any bank globally now had to weigh the risk of doing business with sanctioned entities, since "no bank risks being sanctioned by the USA." This escalation from targeted sanctions to systemic secondary-sanctions risk was framed as a decisive trigger accelerating de-dollarization efforts elsewhere, most notably prompting European attempts at workaround infrastructure like INSTEX, designed to circumvent dollar-clearing chokepoints entirely. The chapter's title — "The Endgame Has Begun" — signaled the report's view that weaponization was reaching a tipping point.

**2024** marked the starkest escalation of the argument, moving from banking fines and secondary sanctions to outright confiscation of sovereign reserves. Following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, "the Biden administration opted in 2022 to suspend Russia's access to the SWIFT international payment network," and, more consequentially, "the freezing of Russia's currency reserves by the US and the EU has highlighted to the world that redemption of US dollar- or euro-denominated reserves may not be guaranteed." This event was treated as a watershed: it was no longer just about restricting transactional access but about demonstrating that dollar- and euro-denominated reserves themselves carried political risk. The report's conclusion was blunt about the long-term consequences — "This erosion of trust is hard to reverse" — framing the 2022 reserve freeze as the moment that cemented, for many nations, the strategic case for reducing dollar exposure and increasing gold holdings as an apolitical store of value.

Across the three years, the argument thus evolved from documenting isolated sanctions enforcement (2019), to identifying systemic secondary-sanctions coercion as an "endgame" trigger (2020), to treating outright reserve confiscation (2024) as the decisive, trust-destroying escalation that accelerates the eastern push toward gold and away from dollar hegemony.
