# Gold-Inflation Correlation

The Gold-Inflation Correlation describes the statistical relationship between the price of gold and the rate of inflation, a relationship that the In Gold We Trust reports argue is not constant but becomes powerfully positive once inflation reaches extreme levels. This concept is significant because it underpins the report's broader case for gold as a monetary asset: in ordinary times the correlation is modest, but in episodes of runaway price increases, gold's behavior shifts decisively, making it a barometer — and potential hedge — for severe currency debasement.

## How the argument evolved

**2009** introduced the concept with a statistical foundation. The report established that "the development of the gold price is strongly correlated with inflation as soon as inflation hits extreme levels," grounding this claim in a long-run dataset: the monthly correlation coefficient between gold and the rate of inflation stood at 0.48 from 1971 to 2009. Crucially, the analysis distinguished between normal and crisis periods, showing that during the high-inflation years of 1978 to 1982 — the late-1970s inflationary spiral — this coefficient jumped to 0.76, a marked strengthening of the relationship. This distinction became the chapter's central thesis: gold does not simply track inflation at all times, but its correlation intensifies dramatically as inflationary pressures become extreme.

**2010** carried this argument forward into the "Inflation Vs Deflation" chapter, restating the core finding almost verbatim — "the gold price correlates very strongly with inflation as soon as the latter hits extreme values" — and again citing the 1978–1982 correlation coefficient of 0.76 as the key evidentiary anchor. What the 2010 treatment added was historical depth and drama: it invoked the Weimar hyperinflation as a further, more extreme illustration of the phenomenon, noting that "by November 1923 the price had shot up to 87 trillion Mark." By pairing the statistical correlation data from the late 1970s with the qualitative historical extreme of Weimar Germany, the report broadened its evidentiary base from a single quantifiable episode to a pattern replicated across different currency and monetary regimes. This move served to generalize the thesis beyond one historical dataset, suggesting that the relationship between gold and inflation is a structural feature of extreme monetary environments rather than a one-off statistical coincidence.

Across both years, the argument's core logic remained stable and was reinforced rather than revised: gold's correlation with inflation is weak-to-moderate in normal conditions but becomes strong and reliable precisely when inflation turns extreme — the scenario in which investors most need protection. The consistency of the 0.76 figure and the near-identical phrasing between the two chapters shows the report treating this as an established, load-bearing pillar of its inflation narrative, while the addition of the Weimar reference in 2010 signaled an effort to situate the statistical finding within a richer historical narrative of currency collapse.
