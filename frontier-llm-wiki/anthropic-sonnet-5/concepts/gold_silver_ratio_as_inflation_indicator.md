# Gold-Silver Ratio as Inflation Indicator

The gold-silver ratio (G/S ratio) — the number of ounces of silver needed to buy one ounce of gold — is treated in the In Gold We Trust reports as a barometer of the tug-of-war between inflation and deflation. Because silver carries both monetary and industrial demand characteristics while gold is almost purely a monetary and safe-haven asset, the relative movement of the two metals reveals shifting momentum between reflationary and disinflationary forces. A falling ratio is read as a bullish signal for gold (and for rising inflation), while a rising ratio warns of disinflationary pressure and caution for gold investors.

## How the argument evolved

The concept was introduced in **2014**, in the context of the broader "monetary tectonics" framework describing the interaction between inflation and deflation. Here the report established the foundational logic: silver's hybrid nature — "monetary demand for it on the one hand and industrial demand on the other" — makes the gold/silver ratio "an excellent indicator for the interaction between inflation and deflation." The report even proposed renaming it the "deflation/reflation" ratio, cementing its interpretive role beyond a simple relative-value metric.

By **2015**, the framework was refined into an actionable signal. The report reiterated the ratio as "an excellent indicator of the interaction between inflation and deflation," but now explicitly tied its direction to investor implications: "A rising G/S ratio is thus a warning signal for gold investors," since it reflects disinflationary momentum, whereas a falling ratio signals reflation and supports higher gold prices. This year also introduced the mean-reversion idea — that "a reversion to the mean has to take place over the long term" — framing the ratio not just as a coincident indicator but as one with long-term corrective tendencies.

In **2016**, the analysis became more statistically grounded, using the ratio's trend as a probabilistic tool for calling gold bull markets. The report argued that "a sustainable rally in the gold price is improbable if the gold/silver ratio rises concurrently," while "a declining g/s ratio significantly increases the probability that gold has entered a bull market." A renewed downtrend was explicitly linked not only to gold's price outlook but to "an increase in the momentum of price inflation as well," tightening the connection between the ratio and broader inflation dynamics.

By **2020**, amid the "status quo of inflation dynamics" discussion, the same statistical logic was reaffirmed almost verbatim: "a sustained rise in the price of gold is unlikely if the gold/silver ratio rises at the same time," and "a falling G/S ratio significantly increases the probability of a bull market in gold." The report cited a concrete recent move — a reversal "from 125 to 100" — as empirical confirmation of this bullish outlook for gold, silver, and inflation simultaneously, demonstrating the durability of the framework first laid out six years earlier.
