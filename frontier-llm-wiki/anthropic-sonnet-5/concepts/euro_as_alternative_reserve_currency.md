# Euro as Alternative Reserve Currency

The euro occupies a unique position in the global monetary order as the only currency to have achieved meaningful reserve status alongside the US dollar. In the "In Gold We Trust" reports' narrative of de-dollarization, the euro functions as a "neutral" outlet for nations seeking to reduce dollar dependence, yet its trajectory is repeatedly shown to be constrained by Europe's political alignment with Washington. This tension — between the euro's technical usability as a reserve and settlement currency and its geopolitical entanglement with the US-led bloc — makes it a central, if ambivalent, case study in the broader remonetization and dollar-alternative debate.

## How the argument evolved

**2021** established the euro's role as the primary beneficiary among alternative currencies in the early de-dollarization trend. The report observed that "the euro always benefits as a neutral third currency when members of the anti-US dollar club do not want to resort to their own currencies," positioning the euro as a convenient middle ground for countries wary of the dollar but unwilling to transact directly in each other's national currencies. This was backed by concrete reserve data: "the share of the euro has risen from 20.5% to 21.2%" in global reserves, a modest but notable gain. Even so, the report was careful to frame this within a hierarchy that remained firmly dollar-dominated, noting that the euro "has always ranked second by some margin" — a clear reserve-currency runner-up rather than a genuine rival.

**2023** sharpened this picture considerably amid the accelerated de-dollarization narrative surrounding the Russia-Ukraine war and Western sanctions. The euro's practical relevance was underscored by its selection as the settlement currency for a major bilateral deal: "the euro was chosen by China and Russia in early 2022 as the settlement currency for their huge gas deal," demonstrating that even geopolitical rivals of the West saw the euro as usable and credible for large-scale trade settlement. The report reaffirmed the euro's standing as uniquely successful among dollar alternatives, stating that "the euro is the only currency that has managed to achieve significant reserve status next to the US dollar so far" — elevating it above other contenders like the yuan in the reserve-currency conversation. Yet this same chapter introduced a crucial caveat that tempered the euro's independence: "by blocking Russia's foreign reserves, Europe has clearly aligned itself with the Western camp led by the US." This action signaled to non-aligned and adversarial nations that the euro, despite its reserve appeal, remains politically tethered to US-led sanctions policy — a liability that could limit its future attractiveness as a truly neutral reserve alternative, even as its practical usage in specific transactions continued to grow.
