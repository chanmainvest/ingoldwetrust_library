# Fiat Currency Depreciation vs Gold

Fiat Currency Depreciation vs Gold is the framing device at the heart of the 2010 In Gold We Trust report: rather than treating a rising gold price as an asset "appreciating," the report inverts the lens and interprets it as paper money losing purchasing power. Gold, in this reading, functions as a fixed measuring stick, while the world's currencies are the variable that erodes against it. This reframing is significant because it shifts the analytical question from "why is gold going up?" to "why is money going down?", a distinction with direct implications for how investors should hedge and how technical trends should be read.

## How the argument evolved

The thesis is introduced at the outset in **2010**'s introduction, which presents a basket of fiat currencies exhibiting a persistent downward trend against gold. The report states plainly that "it is not gold that appreciates, but paper money that depreciates," and observes that this decline is not erratic but structurally consistent — "the downward trend is clearly intact and is in fact right on the trend line." This sets the conceptual foundation: gold's price behavior is to be read as a mirror image of currency weakness rather than as a speculative or commodity-driven rally.

The same **2010** report's technical analysis chapter then extends this idea from a single "basket" abstraction into a global, currency-by-currency phenomenon. Here the argument is broadened and made empirically concrete: the downward trend against gold is shown to be "intact in almost all paper currencies around the world," with the chapter explicitly naming the US dollar, the Swiss franc, the Chinese yuan, the Indian rupee, the British pound, the euro, and the Australian dollar as all sinking relative to gold. This universality is the chapter's key contribution — it moves the concept beyond a US-dollar-centric story (the typical framing of gold bugs at the time) into a claim about fiat money as a category. The chapter crystallizes this with a memorable aphorism, reframing exchange-rate stability as illusory: "Currencies don't float, they just sink at different rates." In doing so, the technical analysis section converts the introduction's conceptual claim into a chart-based, cross-currency technical pattern, giving the depreciation thesis both a narrative and a visual, trend-line-based proof across major world currencies.

Taken together, the two chapters of the 2010 report establish a two-step construction of the idea: first asserting the principle (gold is the fixed point, currencies are depreciating) and then substantiating it technically across nearly every major currency pair, rather than treating it as a dollar-specific weakness. This early articulation lays the groundwork for the report's recurring later use of "currency depreciation" as the default lens through which subsequent years' gold price action would be interpreted.
