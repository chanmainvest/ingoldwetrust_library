# Second Inflation Wave Risk

Second Inflation Wave Risk refers to the In Gold We Trust reports' recurring warning that the inflationary episode of the early 2020s may not be a one-off event, but rather the first of two waves — echoing the historical pattern of the 1970s, when an initial inflation surge was followed by an even larger secondary spike. The concept matters because it reframes disinflation or falling headline CPI prints not as a resolution of the inflation problem, but as a lull before a potentially larger and more damaging resurgence, with direct implications for gold as a long-term store of value.

## How the argument evolved

**2023** marked the first articulation of this risk. Even as the initial post-pandemic inflation surge appeared to be cooling, the report flagged "signs that indicate that a second, probably even more pronounced, wave of inflation is already building up, to hit after the current wave of inflation has subsided." Crucially, this was not framed as a minor echo of the first wave: the report explicitly warned that "the phenomena of this secondary wave may be quantitatively more important than those of the primary wave," setting up an asymmetric risk narrative in which the true inflationary danger still lay ahead.

**2024** built directly on this foundation, moving from a general warning to a more structurally grounded case. The report drew an explicit historical parallel, stating that "a second wave of inflation like that in the 1970s is becoming increasingly likely." This time, the argument was anchored in concrete structural drivers rather than cyclical signals alone: fiscal strain and demographic transition. The report pointed to "the imminent wave of retirements among baby boomers" as a force that "will put a significant strain on budgets in many countries," implying that aging populations would push governments toward higher deficits, more social spending, and ultimately renewed price pressures. This structural framing allowed the 2024 chapter to sharpen the risk assessment considerably, concluding that "the risk of a renewed acceleration in inflation is significantly higher than that of a permanent and significant undershooting of the inflation target." In other words, by 2024 the report had shifted from flagging an emerging possibility to asserting a clear directional bias: the greater danger facing policymakers and investors was too much inflation returning, not too little.

Across the two years, the narrative thus evolved from an early-warning signal based on emerging "signs" to a more fully developed thesis grounded in fiscal and demographic fundamentals, with the 1970s serving as the explicit historical template. The throughline connecting both chapters is the insistence that any apparent victory over inflation should be treated with skepticism, since the underlying pressures — now identified as structural rather than merely transitory — remain capable of producing a second, larger wave.
