# Euro as Dollar Alternative

The euro is presented in the In Gold We Trust reports as one of the leading candidates to challenge the US dollar's dominance in global trade, energy pricing, and reserve holdings. As an explicitly supranational currency not controlled by any single nation-state, it embodies Europe's aspiration for monetary sovereignty and offers other powers — notably China and Russia — a strategic hedge against dollar dependence. The concept matters because it frames the broader de-dollarization narrative: if the dollar's hegemony is to be challenged, the euro is a natural, if imperfect, contender.

## How the argument evolved

The theme first surfaces in **2019**, framed through the lens of European political frustration with dollar dependence. EU officials are quoted arguing for a strengthened international role for the euro, particularly in energy trade, with the striking complaint that "it is absurd that Europe pays for 80% of its energy import bill – worth 300 billion euro a year – in US dollars." This is tied to a broader vision of European sovereignty, encapsulated in the call for "the euro must become the face and the instrument of a new, more sovereign Europe." At this stage the argument is essentially political and aspirational: the euro as a tool for Europe to assert independence from Washington's monetary and financial leverage.

By **2020**, the argument deepens and is placed within the larger historical and geopolitical context of de-dollarization. The report traces the euro's origins as a deliberate project to create an international, non-national currency alternative to the dollar, noting that "Europe embarked on a path that reached its first destination in 1999 in the form of the euro." Crucially, this chapter elevates the euro's significance beyond a European concern, framing it as "explicitly international and cannot be controlled by any one nation state" — a feature that makes it attractive to non-Western powers seeking alternatives to dollar-based systems. This is illustrated by China's support for the euro during its existential crisis, with the report posing the question of "why China stood by the euro in its greatest crisis," implying that China views a viable euro as strategically valuable in counterbalancing dollar hegemony. Thus, the 2020 treatment shifts the euro from a purely European political project into a piece of the global de-dollarization puzzle, aligning it with Russian and Chinese interests in reducing dollar reliance.

Across the two years, the narrative moves from a regional grievance about dollar-denominated trade costs toward a more geopolitical framing in which the euro's non-national character makes it a natural — if still incomplete — alternative reserve and settlement currency in a multipolar monetary order.
