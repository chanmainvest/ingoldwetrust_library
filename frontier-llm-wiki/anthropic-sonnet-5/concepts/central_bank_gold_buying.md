# Central Bank Gold Buying

Central Bank Gold Buying refers to the accumulation of gold reserves by monetary authorities worldwide, a trend the In Gold We Trust reports treat as both a barometer of waning confidence in the US dollar-based monetary order and a structural pillar of gold demand. Far from a marginal technical detail, sustained and record-setting official-sector purchases are presented as evidence of a deeper geopolitical and monetary realignment, with gold increasingly viewed as a strategic reserve asset outside the dollar system.

## How the argument evolved

The theme first enters the reports' analysis in **2019**, framed within the broader narrative of de-dollarization. Here, central bank gold buying is introduced almost as a symptom: the report observes that "global central banks have recently bought more gold than they have in decades," tying this directly to a slow but visible erosion of trust in the US dollar. The emphasis at this stage is qualitative and geopolitical rather than statistical — gold purchases are cited as proof that "gold plays a major role in this slow departure from the US dollar," positioning official demand as part of a wider institutional shift away from dollar hegemony, particularly among European and emerging-market actors seeking alternatives to dollar-denominated reserves.

By **2024**, the argument has matured into a much more data-driven and geographically specific account. Rather than speaking broadly of central banks as a bloc, the report identifies concrete national actors driving the trend, with demand "supported from the global East, especially China (225t) and Singapore (77t)," and Poland also emerging as "a significant purchaser" with 103 tonnes. This shift in emphasis — from a generalized dollar-skepticism narrative to specific, quantified national buying patterns — reflects how central bank gold accumulation had, by this point, become a persistent and measurable feature of the gold market rather than a nascent trend. The 2024 report underscores the momentum behind this buying by noting that "central bank gold demand set a new record for a first quarter," suggesting that what began in 2019 as an emerging signal of de-dollarization had, five years later, solidified into sustained, record-breaking institutional demand. The inclusion of both Western-adjacent (Poland) and Eastern (China, Singapore) buyers also implies a broadening of the phenomenon beyond any single geopolitical bloc, reinforcing the idea that gold's role as a reserve asset is being reasserted across a diverse set of monetary authorities.

Taken together, the two chapters trace an arc from an initial, thesis-level observation about central banks rediscovering gold amid dollar distrust, to a later, empirically grounded confirmation that this buying has become an entrenched, record-setting feature of global gold demand — reinforcing the reports' broader case for gold's structural relevance in the international monetary system.
