# China Exporting Deflation

"China Exporting Deflation" refers to the thesis, developed across the In Gold We Trust reports, that falling Chinese producer and export prices — reinforced by a weakening renminbi and structural economic imbalances — are transmitted abroad as disinflationary or outright deflationary pressure on global goods markets. The concept matters because it complicates simple inflation narratives: even as central banks battle domestic price pressures, China's internal price dynamics can act as a countervailing force, shaping the broader monetary and gold-market backdrop that the reports track closely.

## How the argument evolved

**2024** introduced the idea in the context of currency depreciation. The report observed that "the significant fall in Chinese export prices is having a dampening effect on inflation rates," framing China as a country that "is exporting lower prices to the rest of the world." This was tied explicitly to currency weakness, noting that "the renminbi lost almost 15% against the US dollar by the end of March 2024." At this stage the argument was presented cautiously, acknowledging that the disinflationary effect was real but that "risks of reversal exist" — leaving open the possibility that this dampening force on global inflation could fade or reverse.

**2025** hardened and deepened the thesis. Rather than a temporary currency-driven effect, the report now pointed to a structural condition: "producer prices have been in deflationary territory again since mid-2024," leading to the direct conclusion that "China is therefore exporting deflation." The causes cited expanded beyond the exchange rate alone to include "persistent structural imbalances" within the Chinese economy and the compounding effect of tariffs, alongside the continuation of a "weak renminbi." The 2025 framing was also more forward-looking and emphatic in its global implications, stating that these dynamics "are likely to exert disinflationary, if not deflationary, pressure on global markets." Where 2024 treated the phenomenon as a notable but reversible dampening effect, 2025 elevated it to a persistent structural export of deflation with clear global market consequences.

Taken together, the two chapters trace a progression from an observed correlation (weak currency, falling export prices, muted global inflation) to a firmer causal and structural claim (deflationary producer prices plus imbalances equal an ongoing export of deflation). This evolution mirrors the broader arc of the inflation-trend chapters, which use China's price behavior as a recurring barometer for whether global disinflationary or inflationary forces are likely to dominate — a question with direct bearing on real interest rates, currency valuations, and the investment case for gold.
