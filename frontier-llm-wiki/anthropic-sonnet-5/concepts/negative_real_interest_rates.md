# Negative Real Interest Rates

Negative real interest rates occur when nominal yields fail to compensate investors for inflation, leaving the "real" return on cash and bonds below zero. Across the In Gold We Trust reports, this condition is treated as one of the most powerful structural tailwinds for gold: it erodes the appeal of holding cash and fixed income, distorts risk perception, and pushes capital toward tangible, non-yielding stores of value. The concept evolves from a cyclical by-product of crisis-era rate cuts into a deliberate, long-term policy tool for managing sovereign debt burdens.

## How the argument evolved

**2008** frames negative real rates as an emergency response to financial crisis. With the US central bank left "no option but to react by massively cutting rates," nominal rate cuts combined with rising inflation drove US real interest rates into negative territory. The report treats this as a foundational, almost mechanical setup for gold: negative real rates "should form a base for further increases of the gold price." Crucially, the phenomenon is already noted as international rather than purely American, with "negative real interest rates in USA, China and other countries" signaling a broad, synchronized erosion of returns on cash across major economies.

By **2012**, the argument sharpens and generalizes beyond crisis-driven rate cuts to a deeper structural distortion. The report highlights that yields on German and Swiss government bonds — traditionally considered safe havens — had themselves "turned negative," meaning investors were effectively paying to lend money. This is framed as "the illusion of nominal value": a nominal positive or near-zero yield masks a real loss of purchasing power. The consequence is a shift in investor behavior — in an environment of negative real interest rates, "one has to take risk to preserve one's capital," since safe assets no longer preserve wealth in real terms. This marks an evolution from 2008's view of negative rates as a temporary policy consequence to a systemic condition that pushes even conservative investors into riskier or alternative assets, including gold.

**2021** completes the arc by presenting negative real rates as an entrenched, long-term feature of the monetary landscape rather than a transient crisis response. The report explicitly links deeply negative real rates to debt sustainability, noting that such conditions allowed "the debt-to-nominal-GDP ratio... to shrink back to an acceptable level" — implying that negative real rates function as a deliberate mechanism for eroding the real value of sovereign debt. Looking forward, the report commits to a multi-year outlook, stating belief that "real interest rates will remain in negative territory for the next decade." This is the most explicit statement yet that negative real rates are structural policy, not accident, and it reinforces gold's role as a hedge in "an environment with clearly negative real interest rates." Across the thirteen years spanned by these three chapters, the narrative moves from negative rates as an emergency side-effect (2008), to a systemic distortion of risk and value across sovereign bond markets (2012), to an intentional, decade-long policy backdrop underpinning demand for hard assets (2021).
