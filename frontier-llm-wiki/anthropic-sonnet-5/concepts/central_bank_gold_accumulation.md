# Central Bank Gold Accumulation

Central Bank Gold Accumulation refers to the sustained, and increasingly large-scale, purchase of physical gold by monetary authorities worldwide — a trend the *In Gold We Trust* reports treat as one of the most important structural pillars of the gold market. Framed initially as a side-effect of de-dollarization, it has evolved over the years into a story of record-breaking, "relatively less price sensitive" demand that reports argue effectively puts "a floor under the gold price." Its significance lies not just in the tonnage bought, but in what it signals: a quiet vote of no confidence in the dollar-based reserve system by the very institutions that manage it.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** introduced the theme as a corollary of de-dollarization: countries retreating from dollar-denominated trade were simultaneously seen "expanding their gold reserves noticeably," a strategy endorsed even by mainstream figures like Kenneth Rogoff, who urged emerging markets to accumulate gold.

**2019** sharpened this with hard numbers, noting that central banks "had recently bought more gold in 2018 than they had since 1971," purchasing 651 tonnes that year alone, with global reserves rising 68 percent year-on-year in the first quarter — evidence that buying had accelerated sharply since 2010.

**2020** reinforced the historical framing, describing record purchases "since the global financial crisis" and reiterating that 2018's buying was the largest since the end of the gold standard, casting the trend as a durable, multi-year shift away from currency reserves rather than a one-off spike.

**2021** showed the trend moderating but not reversing: central banks "remained net buyers of gold in 2020," even though purchases fell by almost 60% amid pandemic disruption. The report grounded the motivation in gold's long-term store-of-value role and its crisis performance, keeping the structural narrative intact despite the slowdown.

**2022**, via an interview with Luke Gromen, reframed the driver in geopolitical terms: emerging market central banks were advised to buy physical gold specifically to hedge dollar exposure and reserve-seizure risk, with an expectation of "a steady gold bid from foreign central banks over time" — anticipating that the era's events would later be seen as "really positive for physical gold."

**2023** marked the inflection point the earlier chapters had been building toward. The Introduction and the dedicated de-dollarization chapter both emphasized that central banks had been net buyers "every year since 2009," but 2022 shattered records: purchases rose 152% to over 1,136 tonnes, the most ever recorded, with energy exporters Qatar, Iraq, and the UAE joining the top ten buyers — evidence that the buying base was broadening beyond traditional players toward Asia and energy-exporting nations, mostly outside the West.

**2024** consolidated this into a mature thesis: central banks had "massively accelerated" purchases since 2022, and because this demand is comparatively insensitive to price, it now underwrites the market itself. The Jastram chapter added a reflective, almost cautionary layer, noting the irony that the same institutions "instrumental in creating this global debt bubble" through their policies were now the ones accumulating gold — implicitly preparing for the bubble's resolution, and doing so "even more so" after the Russia sanctions war exposed the risks of dollar-based reserves.
