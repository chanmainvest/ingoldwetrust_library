# De-Dollarization Process

De-dollarization refers to the gradual, multi-decade erosion of the US dollar's dominant role in global trade, reserves, and oil settlement, driven by geopolitical rivals and structural shifts in the international monetary order. The *In Gold We Trust* report treats this not as a sudden rupture but as a slow-moving, largely underappreciated trend with profound implications for gold as a neutral reserve asset outside the fiat system. Its significance lies in framing gold's remonetisation potential against the backdrop of a fraying, dollar-centric order that is being "cautiously and gradually" rebuilt rather than overturned overnight.

## How the argument evolved

**2018** introduced de-dollarization as a structural theme, describing "a cautious and gradual reconstruction of an outdated monetary order" being pursued through the dollar, the yuan, and the euro. The report identified China, Russia, and Iran as the principal actors pushing this shift, and lamented that it was "the most important story no one is reporting." Crucially, the authors set expectations for the timeline from the outset, stating plainly that "the process of 'de-dollarization' is likely to take decades to play out" — a framing that would anchor the concept's treatment in subsequent years.

**2020** revisited the theme under the title "The Endgame Has Begun," signaling that what had been a "long-running, creeping process" was gaining momentum. Rather than predicting an abrupt collapse of dollar hegemony, the report sketched a vision of gradual multipolarity: "a slow path to a multipolar world in which US dollar, euro and renminbi play roles as oil and reserve currencies." This marked a shift in tone from mere observation to a sense of acceleration, while still preserving the multi-decade, evolutionary character established in 2018.

**2024**, in the report's Introduction, returned to de-dollarization as a theme pursued "for many years," reaffirming its persistence as a long-term narrative thread across the report's history. Importantly, this later treatment sharpened the conceptual boundaries of the argument, explicitly rejecting the idea that de-dollarization means the dollar will simply be swapped for another fiat reserve currency: "we do not believe that this process will lead to the US dollar being replaced by another fiat currency as the world's reserve currency." Instead, the dollar was cast as merely the least-flawed contender among failing currencies — "the 'one-eyed' US dollar is king for the time being" among "blind" fiat currencies — a formulation that implicitly strengthens the case for gold rather than any rival national currency as the ultimate beneficiary of dollar decline.

Across the three chapters, the argument thus evolved from identifying the actors and gradual mechanics of de-dollarization (2018), to observing its acceleration toward a multipolar reserve system (2020), to clarifying its ultimate destination as not fiat substitution but a structurally weakened, comparatively-privileged dollar amid a broader monetary reordering (2024) — a trajectory consistently supportive of gold's re-emergence as a neutral store of value.
