# Dow/Gold Ratio

The Dow/Gold Ratio measures the number of ounces of gold required to "buy" the Dow Jones Industrial Average, serving as a barometer of the relative valuation of equities versus gold across secular cycles. In the In Gold We Trust reports, the ratio is treated as a long-horizon technical and valuation signal: when it sits above its historical median, equities are seen as expensive relative to gold, implying that a genuine gold bull market — or equity bear market — should eventually drive the ratio sharply lower. Its significance lies in framing gold not just as an inflation hedge but as a direct counterweight to stock-market valuations, with historical extremes used to project future price targets.

## How the argument evolved

**2012** established the analytical foundation for the Dow/Gold Ratio within the reports' technical analysis of the gold price. At that time the ratio stood "at 7.8x," well "above the long-term median of 5.8x," suggesting equities remained rich relative to gold even after gold's multi-year rally. To underscore how far the ratio could ultimately fall, the report looked back at prior secular extremes: "In 1932, the ratio was 2x, and at the end of the last bull market the ratio was 1.3x." Extrapolating from these historical troughs, the analysis translated the ratio into a concrete gold price target, concluding that "gold would therefore have to rise to USD 6,200/ounce" for the ratio to revisit such extreme lows — a striking illustration of how the metric was used not merely descriptively but as a forecasting tool tied to secular bull-market patterns.

By **2021**, nearly a decade later, the ratio reappears in the report's introductory framing as a signal the authors continue to monitor closely, describing it as something "we pay a lot of attention to." The context had shifted from the post-2011 gold correction to the aftermath of the COVID-19 shock: the Dow/gold ratio had staged a "post-Covid-19 rally," reflecting equities' sharp outperformance relative to gold during the recovery. Yet the report's reading of the chart suggested this rally had run its course, noting that the ratio "seems to have recently completed" its upward move and "could now start resuming its downward trend." This framing echoes the 2012 thesis almost exactly — a belief that elevated equity-to-gold valuations are cyclical anomalies destined to reverse — while updating the empirical anchor point to the post-pandemic market environment.

Taken together, the two chapters show a consistent narrative thread rather than a change in interpretation: the Dow/Gold Ratio is treated as a mean-reverting indicator whose historical extremes (1932, the end of the prior gold bull market, and now the post-Covid rally) mark waypoints for judging whether equities or gold are overvalued. The 2012 chapter supplies the quantitative, historically-grounded price-target methodology, while the 2021 chapter reaffirms the tool's continued relevance and signals that the long-term downward trend — favoring gold over equities — may be poised to resume.
