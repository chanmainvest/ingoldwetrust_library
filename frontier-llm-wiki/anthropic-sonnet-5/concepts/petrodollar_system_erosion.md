# Petrodollar System Erosion

The Petrodollar System Erosion refers to the gradual breakdown of the post-1970s arrangement whereby oil is priced and traded in US dollars, with producer revenues recycled back into dollar-denominated assets. This mechanism has long underpinned structural global demand for the greenback and, by extension, US financial hegemony; its unraveling is significant because it removes a key pillar supporting dollar dominance and opens space for alternative currencies — and potentially gold — to play a larger role in international trade and reserves.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** marked the In Gold We Trust report's first articulation of this theme, framed within the broader de-dollarization narrative. The report observed that the traditional practice of pricing and recycling oil revenues in US dollars was already breaking down as producers diversified their currency exposure. It highlighted the erosion of OPEC's dollar monopoly, noting that "OPEC was made redundant once oil began being priced in both CNY & USD." The report went further, arguing that oil producers had lost incentive to continue the old arrangement, stating that "oil producers have no interest in recycling their revenues as 'petrodollars.'" This shift was framed as so advanced that the once-pressing question of the petrodollar agreement's survival had become moot: "the question of whether there is a threat that the petrodollar agreement could end no longer arises." In effect, the 2017 chapter treated the petrodollar system's decline as an already-unfolding reality rather than a future risk.

By **2023**, the argument had matured and sharpened around concrete geopolitical actors. The report revisited the historical foundation of the system, recalling that "Saudi Arabia, thanks to its cooperation with Washington, has formed the basis for the petrodollar system since the 1970s." Against this backdrop, the 2023 analysis pointed to a decisive structural shift: China's growing use of its own currency in energy trading was presented as evidence that the old bargain was finally giving way. The report concluded starkly that "this system seems to be finally worn out, now that China has long used its own currency in energy trading." Whereas the 2017 chapter emphasized diversification and redundancy in general terms, the 2023 treatment personalized the erosion through the specific Saudi-China dynamic, framing it as a "final showdown" between the incumbent dollar order and rising alternatives. This chronological arc — from an early diagnosis of diversification undermining OPEC's dollar monopoly to a later identification of Saudi-China realignment as the system's terminal stage — shows the report's thesis hardening from cautious observation into a declaration that the petrodollar era is effectively over.
