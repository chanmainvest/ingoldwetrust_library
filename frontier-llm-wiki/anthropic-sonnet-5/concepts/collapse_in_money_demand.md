# Collapse in Money Demand

The "collapse in money demand" refers to a sharp decline in the public's willingness to hold cash balances, a psychological and behavioral shift that the In Gold We Trust reports treat as a decisive—often underappreciated—accelerant of inflation. Rather than viewing inflation purely as a function of money supply growth, this concept emphasizes the demand side of the monetary equation: when confidence in a currency erodes, people rush to convert money into goods or hard assets, and this flight from cash can turn moderate inflation into a runaway spiral. Its significance lies in explaining why inflationary episodes can escalate abruptly and nonlinearly, independent of central bank money-printing decisions alone.

## How the argument evolved

**2015** introduced the concept in a relatively measured, analytical framing. In the chapter on gold and inflation, the report argued that money supply growth by itself is an incomplete explanation for inflation, insisting that "the collapse in the demand to hold money is a crucial factor thereby." Here the emphasis was on establishing money demand as a necessary companion variable to supply-side monetary analysis: "a falling demand for money will intensify the inflation." The 2015 treatment was foundational, positioning the collapse in money demand as an amplifying mechanism rather than a standalone trigger—setting the stage for more dramatic applications in later years.

**2020** revisited and radicalized this idea within a discussion of hyperinflation dynamics. Rather than simply intensifying ordinary inflation, the "sharp decline in the 'demand to hold money'" was now cast as "a major factor in this process" driving inflation toward its most extreme form. The language sharpened considerably: the report described a scenario in which "the demand for money falls precipitously almost to zero, and prices skyrocket upward virtually to infinity." This marked an evolution from a moderating/amplifying factor in 2015 to the central mechanism explaining the tipping point into hyperinflation—a shift reflecting the broader context of extraordinary monetary and fiscal expansion during 2020, which raised the specter of currency confidence breaking down entirely.

Across the five-year span, the argument thus evolved from a foundational corrective to simplistic quantity-theory thinking (2015) into a warning about the endgame of monetary debasement (2020), where the collapse in money demand is not merely a contributing factor but the very engine of hyperinflationary collapse.
