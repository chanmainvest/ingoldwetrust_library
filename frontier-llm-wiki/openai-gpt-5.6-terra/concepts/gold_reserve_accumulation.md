# Gold Reserve Accumulation

Gold reserve accumulation describes the expansion or maintenance of official gold holdings as countries seek a reserve asset not tied to any single national currency. Across the reports, this process is presented as a central feature of de-dollarization: gold’s significance lies in its role as a “super-sovereign reserve currency” and, later, as a neutral monetary alternative being stockpiled by central banks.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** The report framed gold-reserve accumulation as a visible consequence of countries moving away from the dollar in bilateral trade. It observed that “practically all these countries are expanding their gold reserves noticeably,” linking reserve growth to a search for an asset outside the dollar system. Gold was characterized as a “super-sovereign reserve currency”; as a “supra-national” reserve asset, it could play an important role for countries diversifying the monetary basis of trade and reserves.

**2018** The treatment became more focused on the policies of major monetary blocs and powers. China, Russia, and Europe were described as converging in their recognition of gold’s reserve role: “Europe, Russia and China have aligned their gold policy.” Beijing’s actions were singled out—“Beijing continues to build up its gold reserves”—while the broader argument was that these actors recognized gold as “an important element of global reserves.” Reserve accumulation was therefore no longer merely an attribute of individual countries replacing the dollar in trade, but part of an emerging alignment around gold’s place in the international reserve system.

**2020** The account broadened both geographically and quantitatively. The euro-area countries were noted to hold substantial existing reserves—“The euro countries sit on more than 11,000 tonnes of gold”—while Russia and China had “massively increased their gold reserves.” The trend was not confined to the largest powers: “The same applies to smaller countries in Asia and Eastern Europe.” Gold reserve accumulation was thus presented as spanning established holders, large non-Western states increasing their positions, and smaller countries participating in the same direction of travel.

**2023** The report sharpened the monetary meaning of the trend. Gold was presented as “the one true global and neutral money,” and the emphasis moved to the continuing official demand implied by “The relentless stockpiling of gold.” Central banks, particularly in emerging economies, were described as accumulating the metal, with the report noting that “In 2022 global central banks bought 1,136 tons of the yellow metal.” In this formulation, reserve accumulation embodies not only diversification from the dollar but an active preference for gold’s neutrality in an increasingly contested monetary order.
