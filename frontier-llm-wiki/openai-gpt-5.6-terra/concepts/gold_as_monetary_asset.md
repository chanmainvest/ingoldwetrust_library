# Gold as Monetary Asset

Gold as a monetary asset is the report’s central analytical frame for understanding the metal’s role and competitive position. Rather than treating gold chiefly through the lens of industrial use or commodity classification, the reports place it in relation to money and other asset classes. This distinction is significant because it shapes both the scope of the analysis and the interpretation of gold’s changing standing.

## How the argument evolved

**2014** The 2014 report establishes the foundation for this perspective by stating that “we analyze gold primarily as a monetary asset” and explicitly contrasting that approach with viewing it “as an industrial commodity.” Gold is therefore not introduced principally as an input whose importance depends on industrial demand. Instead, the report’s starting point is monetary: gold is assessed in a framework concerned with its role as an asset connected to money.

This framing also supports the report’s stated aim to “perform a holistic analysis of the gold sector.” The term “holistic” indicates that the monetary characterization is not presented as an isolated observation, but as the organizing perspective for examining the sector. By distinguishing monetary analysis from an industrial-commodity approach, the 2014 introduction defines the conceptual basis on which the report approaches gold.

**2015** The 2015 report retains and sharpens this monetary framing. It again declares that “we analyze gold primarily as a monetary asset and not as a commodity.” The wording preserves the earlier distinction while making the contrast especially direct: gold’s primary analytical identity is monetary, not commodity-based.

The 2015 treatment adds a comparative dimension to the established framework. Whereas the prior year defines how gold should be analyzed, the later report assesses its relative position, stating that “The competitive position of gold relative to paper money and other asset classes has improved considerably”. Gold’s monetary character is thus connected to its standing against paper money and competing assets. The development from 2014 to 2015 is not a change in the report’s core definition of gold, but an extension of it: the monetary lens remains constant while the report emphasizes an improved competitive position within the broader landscape of money and asset classes.

Together, the two introductions present gold as more than a commodity category. The reports consistently prioritize its monetary role, use that role to distinguish it from industrial analysis, and, by 2015, explicitly describe gold’s stronger relative position versus paper money and other assets.
