# Inflation and Purchasing Power

Inflation and purchasing power describe the relationship between rising prices and the diminishing capacity of money to buy goods or assets. Across the report chapters, the concept serves as a way to connect consumer-price developments, asset-price movements, and the longer-term erosion of fiat-currency value. The central argument is that inflation’s effects may appear in different places and at different speeds, but both asset and consumer price increases can register declining purchasing power.

## How the argument evolved

**2016** The discussion framed inflation through a distinction between consumer prices and asset prices. It argued that “significant monetary inflation has already taken place,” while observing that it had so far “only” affected asset prices. This framing treated asset-price inflation not as separate from purchasing-power concerns, but as an earlier or differently expressed consequence of monetary conditions. Rising house prices therefore belonged to the same analytical picture as rising food prices: although the affected markets differ, “Both cases are simply a symptom of declining purchasing power.” Purchasing power was consequently presented as the common denominator linking price increases across household necessities and major assets.

This approach also implied that focusing exclusively on consumer-price measures could obscure where inflationary pressures are first visible. If monetary inflation is reflected chiefly in assets, its effect on purchasing power may still be meaningful even before the same dynamic is emphasized through consumer prices. The chapter’s comparison of houses and food made the point concrete: higher prices in either category reduce what money can command.

**2026** The treatment shifted toward the persistence and cumulative character of consumer-price inflation. The chapter emphasized that “a falling inflation rate does not mean that prices are falling,” but “only that they are rising more slowly.” This clarification sharpened the distinction between disinflation and falling price levels: a slower pace of increase does not reverse earlier price increases.

The longer time horizon further developed the earlier purchasing-power argument. Whereas the 2016 chapter highlighted where inflation may emerge—initially in asset prices or in consumer goods—the 2026 discussion focused on the accumulated consequence of continuing price increases. “The loss of purchasing power of fiat currencies becomes even more apparent over a longer period.” Together, the chapters portray purchasing power as both a cross-market measure of inflation’s effects and a cumulative measure of fiat currency’s declining capacity over time.
