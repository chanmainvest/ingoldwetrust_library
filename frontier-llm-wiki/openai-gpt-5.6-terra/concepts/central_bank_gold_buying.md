# Central Bank Gold Buying

Central bank gold buying refers to the accumulation of gold by monetary authorities and other official institutions as part of their reserve management. In the *In Gold We Trust* reports, this shift from official-sector selling to sustained net buying is treated as a structural source of gold demand, one that reinforces gold’s monetary-reserve role and may support prolonged bull-market phases. The argument develops from an initial reversal in the aftermath of the financial crisis into a broader account of de-dollarization, non-Western reserve diversification, and record-scale purchases.

## How the argument evolved

**2010** The report identified a decisive reversal: “the central banks turned into net buyers for the first time in 20 years” in 2009. India and China were “seen on the buying side,” while the Central Bank Gold Agreement (CBGA) “hardly reported any sales.” Rather than treating this as a short-term demand fluctuation, the report framed it as a structural change supportive of a new phase in the gold bull market.

**2011** The reversal was presented as having become established in 2010, when “The central bank sector joined the list of net buyers” and acquired “a total of 90 tonnes of gold last year.” The emphasis shifted toward the composition and durability of demand: “the central banks of emerging countries are still buying.” Thus, official purchasing was increasingly associated with emerging-market reserve managers rather than the prior pattern of Western official sales.

**2020** The report placed central-bank accumulation within the larger de-dollarization narrative. It noted that “since 2010 the purchases by central banks have been an oft-cited argument for a rising gold price,” while stressing that “central banks and other official institutions in general have become net buyers of gold.” The significance was not merely price-related: purchases affirmed that “gold remains an important element of global monetary reserves.”

**2023** The scale of this trend became central to the report’s assessment of gold demand. One chapter stated that “2022 saw the highest gold buying by central banks on record,” with buying reaching “1,136t,” and identified “strong central bank buying into 2023.” The de-dollarization chapter extended the historical claim, arguing that “Central banks as a group have been net buyers of gold every year since 2009.” It also emphasized the geographical character of this demand: “practically all of these central banks are in Asia or the non-West.”

**2024** With annual purchases running at “more than 1,000 tonnes per year,” the report considered whether this buying rate could become a catalyst for another sustained advance in gold. Its conclusion was cautiously constructive: “We think there’s a good chance that there is.” Central-bank demand was therefore portrayed as a potential foundation for a prolonged gold bull market, rather than simply a contemporaneous market influence.

**2025** The persistence of the trend strengthened that interpretation. “Central bank demand for gold not only exceeded 1,000 t for the third year in a row” but “reached a new all-time high of 1,086 t.” Poland (90 t), India (73 t), and China (44 t) led purchases, providing a concrete illustration of the continuing official-sector demand that the reports had traced from the initial post-2009 reversal.
