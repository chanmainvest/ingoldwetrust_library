# Renminbi Internationalization

Renminbi internationalization is China’s effort to expand the yuan’s use beyond its domestic economy—as a trade, payment, financing, and potentially reserve currency. In the reports, this project is significant because it forms part of the broader challenge to US-dollar predominance, while linking China’s trade relationships, commodity markets, payment arrangements, and gold-market infrastructure. The treatment develops from an initial focus on strategic yuan-based market initiatives into a broader account of the currency’s growing practical roles despite continuing capital controls.

## How the argument evolved

**2021** The report framed China’s ambitions in explicitly international and competitive terms: “The aim is to gradually bring the renminbi on a par with the US dollar and the euro.” Trade, oil, and gold-market initiatives were presented as the channels through which China sought to elevate the yuan’s role. The aspiration to create a “petroyuan” placed energy trade at the center of this strategy, while the fact that “China launched a gold fixing in yuan” illustrated an effort to build yuan-denominated reference points in a globally important monetary asset market. Renminbi internationalization was therefore not depicted merely as a change in currency usage, but as an attempt to connect China’s currency with trade flows and the infrastructure of oil and gold markets.

**2023** The argument shifted toward the mechanisms through which the renminbi had already expanded its international presence. The report stated that “the renminbi has emerged as a reserve and trade currency,” but emphasized that this progress had occurred “without lifting capital controls and opening its capital account.” Rather than treating an open capital account as a prerequisite, it identified a set of practical pathways: “import financing, debt payments, payment infrastructures, currency swap lines, and offshore markets.” This broadened the narrative beyond commodity-linked aspirations. Renminbi internationalization appeared as an incremental process, built through financing arrangements and payment networks that could support trade and reserve use even under China’s existing capital-control regime.

**2024** The report returned to the geopolitical and transactional implications of this expansion. China was described as seeking to “raise the renminbi’s status as a potential global reserve currency” while increasing its use in trade and payments. Concrete examples underscored that direction, including “an international LNG transaction settled in renminbi” and the expectation that “imports to the country will be settled in renminbi instead of US dollars.” Compared with 2021’s emphasis on building yuan-based oil and gold-market initiatives, and 2023’s account of supporting infrastructure, the 2024 treatment highlighted settlement itself: renminbi use in real cross-border transactions. Across the three reports, the currency’s internationalization is thus presented as a sustained move from strategic ambition and market construction toward wider operational use in trade, payments, and reserve-currency aspirations.
