# Asset Price Inflation

Asset price inflation describes the rise in the prices of assets as monetary inflation works through the economy, potentially before its effects are visible in consumer-price measures. In the *In Gold We Trust* reports, it is significant because it challenges an inflation assessment based solely on consumer price indices: monetary expansion can already be reducing purchasing power and reshaping prices even where consumer-price inflation appears delayed or subdued.

## How the argument evolved

**2018** The report framed asset price inflation within the contest between inflation and deflation, arguing that money-supply inflation does not move all prices at the same time. Rather, “monetary inflation does not only affect consumer prices but also asset prices,” and its initial effects “would first shift the prices of assets.” Consumer-price inflation, by contrast, was presented as appearing only after a delay. This sequencing made asset markets central to understanding inflation dynamics: an exclusive focus on consumer prices risks overlooking where monetary inflation first becomes visible.

The chapter also directly questioned the adequacy of conventional measurement. Because “consumer price indices do not capture asset price inflation,” an inflation narrative based on those indices alone may understate the effects of monetary expansion. Asset-price movements therefore function in the report’s argument as a separate and earlier channel through which monetary inflation can manifest, rather than as a phenomenon fully represented by consumer-price data.

**2020** The later report sharpened this distinction by explicitly separating monetary inflation from consumer-price inflation. It stated that “enormous monetary inflation has in fact already taken place” and that this was reflected especially in “skyrocketing asset prices.” In this formulation, rising asset prices were not merely a possible preliminary consequence of money-supply expansion; they were presented as the particularly prominent reflection of recent monetary inflation.

At the same time, the report connected both forms of inflation to a common consequence: “In both cases there is a reduction in purchasing power.” The evolution from 2018 to 2020 thus moves from a timing argument—asset prices rise first while consumer prices lag—to a stronger diagnosis that substantial monetary inflation may already be evident in asset markets. Asset price inflation becomes essential for recognizing inflationary effects that consumer-price indices may not capture, while retaining purchasing-power reduction as the shared economic outcome.
