# Central Bank Gold Purchases

Central bank gold purchases describe official-sector additions to gold reserves, a source of demand that the *In Gold We Trust* reports connect to reserve diversification, changing monetary conditions, and the perceived advantages of holding physical gold. Across the corpus, the theme develops from isolated purchases by major institutions into a broader account of sustained buying associated with de-dollarization, demand from the “global East,” and geopolitical sanctions.

## How the argument evolved

**2007** The report identified “Recent purchases by major central banks outside of CBGA II,” establishing official purchases as a notable development beyond the framework governing certain European central-bank gold sales. At this stage, the emphasis was concise: “major central banks outside of CBGA II” were entering the market as buyers, signaling that central-bank activity could not be understood only through the lens of sales agreements.

**2020** The treatment became more explicitly tied to the international reserve system. The report states that “the dollar share of foreign reserves fell” while central banks accumulated gold, including that “central banks bought more gold in 2018.” Rather than presenting purchases as a short-lived event, it characterizes them as “large net purchases that began after the global financial crisis.” Gold buying is therefore framed as part of a longer-running response to shifts in reserve composition and the perceived role of the dollar.

**2024** The reports added both a regional dimension and a geopolitical rationale. In the discussion of inflation, “Central bank demand was supported from the global East,” with substantial buying identified from China—“especially China (225t) and Singapore (77t)”—while “Poland (103t) was also a significant purchaser.” This account broadens the earlier de-dollarization narrative: official-sector demand is not merely an aggregate trend, but one supported by named purchasers across different regions.

In the later 2024 discussion of Roy Jastram’s work, “this central bank gold buying” is presented as ongoing and grounded in the qualities of the asset itself. The report argues that “central banks are fully aware of the advantages of physical gold” and says they “continue their purchases, even more so after the sanctions war.” Taken together, the reports’ argument evolves from observing purchases outside formal European arrangements to interpreting central-bank accumulation as a durable feature of a changing reserve landscape—one shaped by post-crisis dynamics, regional demand, and the strategic value assigned to physical gold amid sanctions.
