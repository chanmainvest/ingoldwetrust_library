# Federal Reserve Average Inflation Target

The Federal Reserve’s average inflation target is presented as a commitment to achieve 2% inflation on average over time, rather than merely reaching 2% at a single point. Its significance lies in the implication that inflation overshoots must be followed by below-target inflation if the average is to be restored. Across the reports, this arithmetic becomes a way of questioning whether the stated target remains meaningful in practice.

## How the argument evolved

**2023** The report described a change in the Federal Reserve’s framework: “the Federal Reserve adjusted its inflation target to average 2% inflation over an undefined period.” The undefined time horizon was central to the critique. If elevated inflation was to be offset in order to preserve a 2% average, the adjustment could not be achieved simply by returning inflation to the target; it would require a sustained period below it.

The report quantified the scale of that requirement following recent high readings. It argued that “inflation would have to average 1.1% over the next 6 ½ years.” Thus, the average-inflation framework was portrayed not as a neutral restatement of a 2% objective, but as a target whose fulfillment imposed a demanding and prolonged disinflationary condition. The gap between the formal average and the inflation path required to attain it highlighted the practical difficulty of treating prior overshooting as something that would later be fully reversed.

**2024** The following report carried the argument forward while anchoring it more explicitly in the framework’s starting point: “an average inflation target of 2% since August 27, 2020.” It again emphasized that past overshooting changes what compliance with an average target entails. Rather than allowing inflation merely to settle at 2%, the arithmetic of the average required subsequent inflation below that level.

The updated calculation stated that “the average inflation rate would now have to be 1.3%.” Compared with the earlier discussion, the report’s focus had shifted from illustrating the severity of the required adjustment to drawing a broader conclusion about the target itself. Because the required below-target period had not been treated as the operative policy objective, the report concluded that “the Federal Reserve’s inflation target was – indirectly and implicitly – raised.” The concept therefore developed into an argument about the distinction between a formally retained 2% average target and the effective inflation tolerance implied by not offsetting earlier inflation above that rate.
