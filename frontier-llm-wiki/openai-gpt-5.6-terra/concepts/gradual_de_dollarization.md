# Gradual De-Dollarization

Gradual de-dollarization is the report’s description of a slow restructuring of the international monetary order, not a sudden displacement or collapse of the dollar. Its significance lies in the report’s consistent rejection of imminent-collapse narratives: the transition is presented as a long-term realignment, potentially reshaping the relative roles of major currencies and the broader world order over decades.

## How the argument evolved

**2018** The report established the core framing of de-dollarization as an extended process rather than a near-term break with the existing system. It argued that “the process of ‘de-dollarization’ is likely to take decades to play out,” placing the concept in a long historical timeframe. Rather than depicting the dollar’s position as ending abruptly, the chapter described “a cautious and gradual reconstruction of an outdated monetary order” as underway. This formulation made gradualism central: de-dollarization was not a single event, but a measured reconstruction of the monetary framework.

**2021** The report reaffirmed that pace while developing a more explicitly multipolar picture of the potential destination. It stated that “the process of de-dollarization is a gradual, slow one,” preserving the earlier insistence that the transition should not be confused with an immediate dollar collapse. At the same time, it suggested a future in which “three world currencies – the euro, the renminbi, and the dollar – sharing the spotlight.” The emphasis therefore shifted from reconstruction alone toward the possibility of shared monetary prominence, with the dollar remaining part of the picture rather than simply disappearing from it.

**2023** The treatment became more emphatic about the reality of the process while retaining its caution about timing. The report stated that “the process of de-dollarization is in fact happening,” but immediately framed its progression through the image that “Tectonic plates move very slowly.” This combination sharpened the argument: de-dollarization was neither hypothetical nor imminent in its final effects. Instead, it was portrayed as a long-running realignment in which “more and more countries see the possibility of a world order.” Across the three chapters, the report thus developed a consistent thesis of slow but consequential change—from cautious reconstruction, to a possible three-currency arrangement, to an ongoing shift in how countries envision world order.
