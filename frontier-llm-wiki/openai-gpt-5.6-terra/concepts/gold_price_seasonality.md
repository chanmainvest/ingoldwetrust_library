# Gold Price Seasonality

Gold price seasonality describes the recurring tendency for gold to perform differently at particular points in the calendar year, reflecting demand cycles and historical monthly patterns. In the *In Gold We Trust* reports, this pattern is significant because it offers a recurring framework for interpreting short-term price behavior: strength is associated chiefly with the fourth and first quarters, while the middle of the year is portrayed as comparatively weaker.

## How the argument evolved

**2008** The report introduced seasonality as a pronounced and recurring feature of the gold market, rooted broadly in “demand cycles and historical monthly performance.” Its formulation was statistical rather than tied to a particular regional demand source: the “pronounced seasonality of the price” was said to have held in “75 to 80% of the cases.” The practical conclusion was clear: “the fourth and the first quarter show the best performance of the year.” Thus, seasonality was presented as a historically observable tendency, useful for placing annual gold-price movements in a calendar-based context rather than treating them as wholly random.

**2011** The treatment became more specific about the demand mechanisms thought to produce the recurring pattern. The report described “the profound seasonality” as being “caused by the so-called wedding season as well as the Diwali festival in India.” It also connected this period to jewellery restocking, making Indian wedding and festival demand central to the explanation for the seasonal tendency. Although the underlying conclusion remained consistent with the earlier report—“the fourth and the first quarter of the year show the best performance”—the argument had evolved from a historical observation into a demand-led account of why the pattern might recur.

**2012** The report retained the emphasis on Indian and jewellery-related demand but developed the calendar profile into a fuller annual sequence. Gold “tends to perform best in the fourth and the first quarter,” while the subsequent periods were characterized more cautiously: it “would correct substantially in the second quarter” and “move sideways in the third quarter.” This framing made seasonality not merely a statement about favorable quarters, but an interpretive template for potential midyear weakness and consolidation. Across the three reports, the concept therefore progressed from a recurring historical pattern, to an explanation centered on Indian seasonal demand, and finally to a more complete description of the expected rhythm across all four quarters.
