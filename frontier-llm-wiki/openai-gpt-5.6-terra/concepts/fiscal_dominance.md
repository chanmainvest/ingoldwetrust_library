# Fiscal Dominance

Fiscal dominance describes a condition in which fiscal needs—particularly deficit financing—come to exert growing influence over monetary policy. In the In Gold We Trust reports, it matters because it frames the relationship between governments and central banks as increasingly intertwined, with the capacity to tighten monetary policy constrained by the scale and persistence of fiscal deficits. The concept therefore highlights a shift away from an emphasis on “the political independence of central banks” toward fiscal policy as a decisive force in setting monetary conditions.

## How the argument evolved

**2021** The report introduced fiscal dominance as a development accelerating the institutional and practical convergence of fiscal and monetary policy. It stated that “This new fiscal dominance is accelerating the merging of monetary and fiscal policy,” placing deficit financing at the center of this merger. As deficits expand, “a successively higher share of the deficits must be financed,” suggesting that the fiscal side increasingly demands monetary accommodation or support. The significance of this process was not presented merely as a technical matter of public finance: it raised questions about “the political independence of central banks.” Fiscal dominance thus appeared as a challenge to the separation between governments’ financing requirements and central banks’ monetary-policy mandates.

**2024** The report developed this argument by focusing on the conflict between fiscal deficits and attempts at monetary tightening. It described “significant deficits thwarting the tightening efforts of the central banks,” presenting deficits not simply as a background condition but as an active counterweight to restrictive policy. The framing became more explicit and forward-looking: “we are entering an era of fiscal dominance.” In this account, the hierarchy between policy areas is changing. Rather than monetary authorities independently determining the policy stance while fiscal policy adjusts around it, “It will be fiscal policy that sets the pace for monetary policy.”

Taken together, the two treatments trace a progression from the merging of monetary and fiscal policy to a clearer claim that fiscal conditions can direct the monetary-policy path. The 2021 discussion identifies the mechanism—rising deficit-financing needs and pressure on central-bank independence—while the 2024 discussion emphasizes its consequence: deficits can limit tightening and make fiscal policy the pace-setting force. Fiscal dominance is therefore presented as an evolving policy regime in which fiscal requirements increasingly shape the scope and direction of central-bank action.
