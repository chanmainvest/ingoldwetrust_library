# Negative Money Supply Growth

Negative money supply growth describes a contraction in the supply of money and credit rather than its expansion. In the 2023 In Gold We Trust report, this development is treated as historically unusual in the United States and as a warning of economic dislocation, recessionary conditions, and disinflationary pressure rather than a straightforward continuation of the inflation trend.

## How the argument evolved

**2023** The report’s introduction frames the US contraction in particularly stark historical terms. It notes that “money supply growth in the US, calculated on a monthly basis, is negative” and that it has turned negative “on an annual basis for the first time since the Great Depression.” This is not presented as a technical monetary statistic in isolation: the report interprets a falling supply of money and credit as evidence of broader strain, arguing that “A declining money and credit supply is a sure sign of profound economic dislocation.” Negative growth therefore functions as an early warning indicator, suggesting that the economic effects of monetary tightening may be more severe than conventional inflation narratives imply.

Later in **2023**, the discussion of the inflation trend places negative money-supply growth within a wider set of recessionary signals. The report again emphasizes that “money supply growth is negative for the first time since the Great Depression,” while pairing it with “The inverted yield curve and poor consumer sentiment,” described as “already strong signs of an economic slump.” The focus consequently shifts from the exceptional character of monetary contraction to its macroeconomic implications: weaker activity and a potential downturn.

This framing also clarifies the report’s distinction between monetary conditions and inflation outcomes. While inflation may remain an important concern, negative money-supply growth is associated with the prospect that the economy enters a disinflationary phase. The report states the relationship directly: “Recessions are always disinflationary.” In this account, contracting money and credit, an inverted yield curve, and weak sentiment reinforce one another as signals that the prevailing inflation trend could give way to economic weakness and disinflation.
