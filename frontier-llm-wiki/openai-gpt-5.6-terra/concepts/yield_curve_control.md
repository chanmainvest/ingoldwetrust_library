# Yield Curve Control

Yield curve control is presented as a central-bank policy of purchasing government bonds as necessary to limit, or cap, yields. Its significance in the reports lies in its capacity to shape Treasury yields and address financial-market pressure, rather than merely setting a policy rate. The concept is framed as an open-ended form of intervention: “quantitative easing without quantity restrictions.”

## How the argument evolved

**2021** The report introduces yield curve control through the mechanics of bond-market intervention. It describes “a policy of explicit or implicit yield curve control,” under which central banks “buy as many government bonds as needed to cap yields.” This formulation places the emphasis on the policy’s potentially unlimited scale: the operative constraint is not a predetermined amount of bond purchases, but the objective of preventing yields from rising beyond the desired level. Accordingly, the report characterizes the approach as “quantitative easing without quantity restrictions.” Yield curve control is therefore distinguished by its focus on an outcome in the yield market—capped yields—rather than by a stated quantity of asset purchases.

**2023** The later treatment shifts from definition toward prospective application. Yield curve control appears as a possible means of managing Treasury yields and the pressures associated with financial markets, with “Yield curve control measures” discussed in the context of preventing a “(sharp) tightening of yields.” The report’s language also highlights the relationship between this policy and quantitative easing, referring to “QE under the ‘guise’ of yield curve control.” Whereas the 2021 discussion foregrounds the central bank’s willingness to purchase whatever volume of government bonds may be required, the 2023 discussion foregrounds the policy rationale for doing so: avoiding a sharp tightening in yields.

Taken together, the two chapters portray yield curve control as an intervention whose defining mechanism is bond buying in support of a yield objective, and whose prospective role is to contain upward pressure on Treasury yields. The evolution is one of emphasis rather than contradiction. The earlier account establishes the policy’s open-ended purchase logic; the later account situates that logic within a setting of financial-market pressure and the desire to prevent tighter yields. In both treatments, the central feature remains the use of central-bank bond purchases to influence the yield curve.
