# Non-Dollar Oil Settlement

Non-dollar oil settlement refers to arrangements in which oil is paid for with currencies or assets other than US dollars, including yuan, euros, local currencies, and gold. In the reports’ treatment, these arrangements matter because they illustrate concrete efforts to reduce reliance on dollar-based oil trade, moving the de-dollarization discussion from a general theme to specific bilateral payment practices.

## How the argument evolved

**2018** The report presented non-dollar settlement primarily through the actions of China and Iran. China was described in connection with “bilateral oil trading agreements settled in yuan,” placing the yuan at the center of an alternative framework for oil transactions. Iran, meanwhile, was portrayed as diversifying the currencies in which it sold oil: “it was selling oil to China for yuan and to other countries for euros.” The discussion also extended beyond these two countries, noting trading “with Turkey and important US ally South Korea, among others, in euros and/or their own currencies.” Taken together, these examples framed non-dollar oil settlement as a set of bilateral and regional practices rather than a single replacement system. Yuan, euros, and domestic currencies appeared as different means of limiting dependence on dollar settlement in oil trade.

**2023** The report broadened the range of settlement alternatives by highlighting arrangements involving both gold and local currency. Ghana was associated with an effort to “buy oil with gold instead of US dollars,” introducing gold directly as a means of payment for oil rather than merely as a reserve or monetary reference point. Kenya was cited for an arrangement to “purchase oil for Kenyan shillings,” emphasizing a domestic currency route to oil payments. Compared with the 2018 focus on yuan, euros, and other local currencies in bilateral trading, the 2023 examples showed that the report’s argument had expanded to include gold-based payment as well as local-currency purchasing arrangements. The continuing thread was the same: oil payments could be organized through instruments other than the US dollar, whether through major international currencies, national currencies, or gold.
