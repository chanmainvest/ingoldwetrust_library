# Gold-Oil Relative Valuation

Gold-oil relative valuation assesses gold through the amount of oil that one ounce of gold can purchase, offering a commodity-based perspective on whether gold appears fairly valued or expensive. Its significance lies in connecting gold’s relative strength to oil-market conditions, including the potentially supportive inflation and crisis implications of rising oil prices as well as their cost-increasing effects. Across the reports, the framing shifts from gold being fairly valued against oil to gold appearing expensive in a long-term comparison.

## How the argument evolved

**2012** The report treated the gold-oil relationship as broadly balanced. It stated that “One ounce of gold currently buys 15 barrels of oil,” while characterizing this level as the long-term median and concluding that “gold is fairly valued in relation to oil.” The ratio was therefore not presented as signaling an extreme relative mispricing. At the same time, the assessment was not static: gold was said to be “gaining in relative strength again.” This combined valuation with momentum, suggesting that gold’s position versus oil could improve even while remaining consistent with a fair long-term relationship.

**2026** The later report retained the gold-oil ratio as a long-term valuation measure but reached a more cautious conclusion. It identified a historical benchmark of “roughly 17 barrels of oil per ounce of gold” and argued that “gold still looks expensive relative to oil in longterm comparison.” The focus had thus moved from a 2012 judgment of fair value to an emphasis on gold’s relative expensiveness against oil when set beside its longer-run ratio.

The 2026 treatment also gave the oil side of the comparison a broader role. Rather than treating oil solely as the denominator in a valuation ratio, it noted that “Rising oil prices can support gold through inflation and crisis expectations.” Oil-price increases could therefore support gold’s appeal through these channels, even as they carry cost-increasing effects. The concept consequently developed from a relatively straightforward measure of fair value and relative strength into a framework that balances long-term ratio valuation with the macroeconomic implications of changing oil prices.
