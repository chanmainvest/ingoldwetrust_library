# Political Inflationary Bias

Political inflationary bias describes the tendency of political incentives to favor monetary stimulus and money-supply expansion while making restraint or reversal politically costly. In the reports, this asymmetry arises because stimulus can support campaign promises and mitigate recession fears, whereas reducing it can expose policymakers to economic and political pressure. Its significance lies in the argument that inflationary dynamics are not merely technical monetary choices but are shaped by the incentives embedded in the political system.

## How the argument evolved

**2012** The report frames the issue as an “inflationary bias in the political system.” Political incentives encourage stimulus, including through campaign pledges, which “constitute the basic requirements for the expansion of the money supply.” The argument emphasizes the asymmetric political consequences of changing course: expansion is supported by the appeal of stimulus, but withdrawal confronts the danger that “As soon as the stimulus is reduced, the risk of recession increases.” Thus, the political setting creates persistent pressure toward continued monetary accommodation, because the potential costs of reducing stimulus become immediately visible in the form of recession risk.

**2020** The treatment revisits and sharpens the same structural argument through the observation that “Bernholz also recognizes an inflationary bias in the current political system.” As in the earlier discussion, “stimuli and election promises are the basic prerequisite for money supply expansion,” linking expansionary monetary policy to political inducements. The later account makes the distinction between technical capacity and political feasibility especially explicit: “the reduction of the money supply is technically possible without problems, it is politically difficult to implement.” Political inflationary bias therefore appears not as an inability to reverse monetary expansion, but as a reluctance to do so when recession fears and the political appeal of stimulus make reversal difficult.

Across the two treatments, the core thesis remains consistent: political incentives facilitate expansion more readily than contraction. The 2012 account stresses the recession risk associated with reducing stimulus, while the 2020 account clarifies that this is principally a political obstacle rather than a technical one. Together, they portray inflationary bias as a durable feature of a system in which stimulus and promises support money-supply growth, but retrenchment carries politically difficult consequences.
