# Gold Seasonal Headwinds

Gold Seasonal Headwinds describes the historically unfavorable periods for bullish gold expectations identified in the reports’ technical-analysis discussions. Its significance lies in timing: rather than treating seasonal patterns as a permanent verdict on gold, the reports frame them as recurring intervals of weakness that may constrain near-term bullish expectations before a potential reversal or renewed upward phase. Across the two editions, the seasonal window is presented first with particular attention to post-election-year timing and later as part of a broader March-to-July caution period.

## How the argument evolved

**2021** The report’s treatment focuses on a specific seasonal pattern in post-election years. It states that “seasonal headwinds set in from the end of May but reverse again at the beginning of July,” presenting the weakness as a defined, temporary period rather than an open-ended bearish condition. June receives particular emphasis within this framework: “June is – after January – the second weakest month in post-election years.” The implication is that late spring and especially June could be difficult for gold, while the beginning of July historically marked a reversal in the seasonal conditions. This framing makes the calendar relevant to technical expectations, but it also preserves a distinction between a short-lived seasonal obstacle and the subsequent possibility of improving conditions.

**2022** The later report broadens the cautionary seasonal interval. Instead of concentrating on the end of May through the start of July in post-election years, it describes March through July as a span in which “A bullish expectation has rarely paid off in the past between March and July.” This formulation shifts attention from one especially weak month and a post-election-year reversal point toward a wider period in which bullish positioning has historically faced unfavorable odds. The report nevertheless retains the idea that seasonal headwinds need not persist indefinitely. It allows that “the next few weeks could still bring headwinds,” while identifying a possible turning point later in the calendar: “only from August onwards would the start of a new multi-month upward wave” become relevant.

Taken together, the two chapters develop seasonal headwinds as a time-sensitive constraint on bullish gold expectations. The 2021 discussion identifies a late-May-to-early-July pattern, especially through June, whereas the 2022 treatment extends the unfavorable historical backdrop from March into July and places a prospective recovery from August onward. In both cases, the seasonal argument is not simply bearish; it is structured around the prospect that weakness may eventually give way to a new upward phase.
