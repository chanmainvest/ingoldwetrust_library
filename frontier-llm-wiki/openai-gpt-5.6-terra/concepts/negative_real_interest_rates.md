# Negative Real Interest Rates

Negative real interest rates denote a setting in which returns measured in real terms are below zero, undermining the ability of conventional savings instruments to preserve capital. Across the *In Gold We Trust* reports, this condition is treated as significant both for gold’s prospects and for the broader investment landscape: it can erode nominally safe holdings, encourage investors to accept greater risk, and strengthen demand for stores of value.

## How the argument evolved

**2008** The report introduced negative US real interest rates primarily as a monetary and gold-market issue. It argued that aggressive policy easing—described as “massively cutting rates”—had pushed “US real interest rates onto negative ground.” Rather than treating this as an isolated consequence of the financial environment, the chapter presented it as a supportive foundation for gold: negative real rates were seen as a “base for further increases of the gold price.” The emphasis was therefore on the relationship between diminished real returns from interest-bearing assets and the potential for further appreciation in gold.

**2012** The discussion broadened from gold-price support to the practical consequences for capital preservation. The report observed that “the yields of short-term German and Swiss bonds have meanwhile turned negative,” demonstrating that the problem was not confined to US real-rate conditions. In this environment, even assets commonly regarded as conservative ceased to offer dependable protection, because “nominally ‘safe’ assets still incur losses.” The resulting investment dilemma was stated starkly: “one has to take risk to preserve one’s capital.” Negative rates thus became not merely a bullish backdrop for gold, but evidence of an altered financial landscape in which safety, nominal return, and preservation of capital no longer aligned.

**2021** The report extended the argument into a longer-term structural outlook. It noted that “real interest rates were deeply negative” and projected that “real interest rates will remain in negative territory for the next decade.” This persistence mattered because the report connected an “environment with clearly negative real interest rates” to debt reduction and to demand for stores of value. The earlier case for gold-price gains and the later recognition of losses in nominally safe assets consequently converged in a broader interpretation: sustained negative real rates could reshape investor behavior by making preservation of purchasing power and access to stores of value increasingly important.
