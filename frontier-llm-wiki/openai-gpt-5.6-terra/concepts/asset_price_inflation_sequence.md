# Asset Price Inflation Sequence

Asset Price Inflation Sequence describes the Austrian Business Cycle Theory (ABCT) ordering in which inflation first appears in capital-good and asset prices before it reaches consumer prices. Its significance lies in distinguishing an early phase of inflationary change—asset price inflation—from the later emergence of consumer price inflation, thereby emphasizing that rising consumer prices are not presented as the process’s initial manifestation.

## How the argument evolved

**2014** The report introduced the sequence through Austrian Business Cycle Theory’s account of the inflationary process. Its formulation was direct: “the prices of capital goods (= asset price inflation) are the first to increase.” In this framing, capital goods and assets are where inflationary effects appear first, while consumer prices do not move in parallel at the outset. Rather, “consumer price inflation only sets in at a later stage.” The chapter therefore treated asset price inflation as an earlier stage and consumer price inflation as a subsequent one, making timing central to the distinction between the two.

This ordering also gives the concept its analytical character. Inflation is not represented solely through later changes in consumer prices; under the stated ABCT sequence, changes in capital-good and asset prices precede them. The 2014 treatment thus established a staged account: first asset price inflation, then consumer price inflation.

**2015** The following year reaffirmed the same theoretical sequence while connecting it more explicitly to the present. The report stated that “Current developments correspond precisely to the textbook Austrian Business Cycle Theory (ABCT).” This wording moved the argument beyond a general statement of theory and presented current developments as matching its predicted order.

The sequence itself remained unchanged: “the prices of capital goods (=asset price inflation) thus rise first,” whereas “consumer prices begin to rise (=consumer price inflation) only later.” Compared with the prior year, the treatment sharpened the relationship between the theory and observed developments without altering the underlying chronology. Asset-price increases remained the initial inflationary expression in capital goods, and consumer-price increases remained the later phase. Across both chapters, the report’s argument is consistent: the relevant question is not simply whether inflation exists, but where in the sequence it is appearing—first in assets and capital goods, and only later in consumer prices.
