# Triffin Dilemma

The Triffin Dilemma describes the conflict faced by the issuer of a global reserve currency: it must meet international demand for liquidity while also pursuing domestic economic objectives and preserving confidence in its currency. In the reports, the dilemma is presented as a structural feature of the dollar-centric monetary system, in which the United States’ reserve-currency role requires an outward flow of dollars that can create tensions between national and international interests. Its significance lies in showing why global reliance on a single currency can make the issuer’s domestic position inseparable from the world’s need for monetary liquidity.

## How the argument evolved

**2016** The report framed the issue in its classic form: “a nation that issues a global reserve currency is subject to a conflict of interests.” The conflict was defined as one “between short term domestic and long term international goals,” highlighting the mismatch between the issuing country’s own policy priorities and the broader system’s need for reserve-currency liquidity. The report also tied this role to an external-balance requirement, stating that “the issuing nation has to have a current account deficit.” In this formulation, the dilemma rests on the need to supply the world with the reserve currency even where doing so conflicts with the issuer’s domestic or long-term objectives.

**2024** The report revisited the same structural tension through the continuing centrality of the US dollar and the conditions faced by countries that depend on it. “As the US dollar is the global reserve currency, there is international demand for it,” but this demand leaves other countries in a position where they “borrow, save, and trade in a currency they cannot print.” The analysis consequently emphasized not only the issuer’s conflict but also the international dependence created by dollar use. It restated the required outward provision of currency in more explicit trade terms: “the issuing country must be prepared to perpetually export more of its currency than it recoups through trade surpluses.”

Taken together, the treatment develops from a concise statement of the reserve issuer’s conflict into a broader account of a dollar-based system. The earlier discussion foregrounded the tension between domestic and international goals and the associated current-account deficit; the later discussion stressed persistent global dollar demand, the dependence of other countries on a currency they cannot create, and the necessity for the issuer to export currency on an ongoing basis. In both cases, the dilemma remains rooted in the same proposition: fulfilling the global reserve-currency function requires the issuer to accommodate international liquidity needs that may not align with its own domestic interests.
