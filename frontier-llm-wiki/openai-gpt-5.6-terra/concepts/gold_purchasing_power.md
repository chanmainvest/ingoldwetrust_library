# Gold Purchasing Power

Gold purchasing power refers to gold’s capacity to acquire real goods over extended periods, rather than its price in fiat currency alone. Across the reports, this capacity is presented as relatively stable when measured against necessities such as oil and bread, and as a defining feature of gold’s role outside inflated fiat money. The argument matters because it frames gold not as an interest-bearing asset, but as a liquid store of value whose long-run purchasing capacity can endure.

## How the argument evolved

**2009** The report introduced purchasing power through the gold-oil ratio, treating the relationship between an ounce of gold and oil as historical evidence of stability. “The stable purchase power of gold is also reflected in this ratio,” it argued, emphasizing that “one ounce of gold currently buys about the same amount of oil” as it did in several earlier periods. By specifically comparing the then-current ratio with 1945, 1982, and 2000, the discussion grounded gold’s purchasing-power case in a long historical comparison of real commodities rather than in nominal gold-price movements.

**2012** The argument broadened from oil to everyday staples and made the preservation claim more explicit. The report stated that “gold does not only preserve its purchasing power,” illustrating the point with bread: “one ounce of gold would buy 350 loaves of bread.” The conclusion was direct: “the purchasing power of gold remained stable.” This treatment shifted the emphasis from a single commodity ratio to the practical purchasing capacity of gold against basic goods, reinforcing the notion that its value can be assessed in terms of what it buys.

**2018** The report placed this long-run preservation capacity within a monetary context. Gold was described as an asset distinct from “inflated fiat money,” while retaining the practical characteristic that “Gold is a liquid asset.” Its long-horizon argument was also qualified by the absence of yield: “the yellow metal has at least maintained its purchase power” “over long periods of time without interest income.” Purchasing power thus became part of a wider account of gold’s monetary role: it may not generate interest income, but the report presents its liquidity and its capacity to maintain purchasing power over time as central attributes.
