# Central Bank Digital Currencies

Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) are state-issued digital money that the reports present as consequential to the future design of monetary systems. Their significance lies not only in their technological form, but in the policy choices they may enable: the reports connect CBDCs to debates over the dollar’s role, central-bank responses during Covid-19, deeper negative interest rates, and the status of anonymous cash. Across the two years, CBDCs emerge as both a subject of international monetary-system discussion and a possible instrument of more direct monetary control.

## How the argument evolved

**2020** The report situated CBDCs within a broad and unresolved international monetary debate. It referred to “The debate on “digital central bank currencies” (CBDC)” as part of a wider discussion that also included “the question of the US dollar succession.” This framing made CBDCs relevant not simply as a payments innovation, but as an issue connected to the prospective ordering of the monetary system and the future role of the US dollar. During Covid-19, the report also noted support from the Bank for International Settlements for advancing the technology, stating that “the development of CBDCs should be promoted.” CBDCs were therefore presented as a live policy response being encouraged amid a period of disruption, while remaining embedded in larger questions of monetary leadership and de-dollarization.

**2021** The treatment became more concrete about what a CBDC could mean for domestic monetary policy and individual use of money. The report described the possibility that authorities might “introduce a central bank digital currency (CBDC),” emphasizing its character as state-issued digital money rather than merely a topic of systemic debate. It then associated such an introduction with the “implementation of even deeper negative interest rates,” suggesting that CBDCs could widen the practical scope for negative-rate policy. At the same time, the report linked the digital-currency discussion to “pushback against anonymous cash.” This added a dimension of privacy and cash use to the earlier focus on international monetary change: CBDCs could matter not only because of who issues money and how it circulates, but also because they may alter the constraints imposed by physical cash and its anonymity.

Taken together, the reports’ argument develops from CBDCs as a policy initiative within a changing global monetary order to CBDCs as a potentially consequential tool for implementing monetary policy and reshaping the role of cash. The recurring concern is that the adoption of a central-bank digital currency may carry implications extending well beyond digital payments.
