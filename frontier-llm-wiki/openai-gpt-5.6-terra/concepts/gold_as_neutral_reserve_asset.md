# Gold as Neutral Reserve Asset

Gold as a neutral reserve asset is the idea that gold can serve governments and central banks without being tied to the political authority, credit, or currency of a single issuer. Across the *In Gold We Trust* reports, this role becomes increasingly significant in the context of de-dollarization: first as a nonpolitical reserve holding, then as a potential reference asset for a changing monetary order, and ultimately as an alternative to US Treasuries in the international reserve system.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** The report framed gold’s reserve function primarily in terms of political neutrality. Gold “has certain advantages” because it is “a neutral reserve asset held by more than a hundred countries.” Unlike a reserve asset dependent on an individual country or its policy choices, gold was described as nonpolitical: “In that sense, it’s nonpolitical.” This made it relevant not only as a central-bank holding but also as a possible element informing future international currency arrangements.

**2019** The argument was placed more explicitly within de-dollarization. “The answer is de-dollarization,” the report stated, while identifying gold as the reserve asset through which central banks could reduce their reliance on the dollar. Gold was increasingly visible on official balance sheets—“Gold, which many central banks now include in their balance sheets at market value”—and was characterized more strongly as “the only truly neutral asset available to governments and central banks.” Neutrality was therefore no longer merely an intrinsic quality of gold; it became the rationale for official accumulation amid a changing reserve landscape.

**2020** The report extended the concept from reserve diversification toward monetary-system design. Gold was expected to have “a prominent role as a neutral reserve asset” in a new framework, one “that floats in all currencies.” Rather than requiring a fixed gold standard or a single dominant currency, this formulation positioned gold as a floating reference point across currencies. The report also proposed that monetary authorities could “use gold as a reference point for inflation expectations,” giving gold a potential informational and coordinating role in addition to its function as a reserve asset.

**2024** The treatment culminated in a direct comparison between gold and US Treasuries as the de facto reserve asset. The report argued that “the alternative is gold” and that “gold can still replace USTs as the de facto reserve asset.” In this account, gold’s neutrality addresses the single-issuer problem at the center of the Triffin Dilemma: “Gold serving as a neutral reserve asset doesn’t just fix Triffin’s Dilemma, it obviates it completely.” Gold is thus presented not simply as a hedge against dollar dependence, but as an asset capable of supporting a reserve system without requiring one nation’s liabilities to serve the world.
