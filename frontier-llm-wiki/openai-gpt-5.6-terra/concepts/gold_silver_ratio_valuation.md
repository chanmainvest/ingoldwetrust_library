# Gold-Silver Ratio Valuation

Gold-silver ratio valuation assesses silver in relation to gold, using the ratio both to translate a gold-price scenario into an implied silver price and to judge silver’s relative valuation within precious metals. In the report’s treatment, a lower assumed ratio can produce a substantially higher implied silver price when paired with a gold-price target, while an elevated ratio may indicate depressed sentiment and an historically extreme valuation relationship. The concept is significant because it links silver’s prospective price outcomes to gold while also framing the relative risk of further declines.

## How the argument evolved

**2016** The report presented the gold-silver ratio primarily as a scenario-setting mechanism for estimating a potential silver price. It paired “At a gold price of USD 2,300” with “a gold/silver ratio of 40” to derive the conclusion that “the price of silver would stand at USD 57.50.” This framing made the ratio a key variable in translating a view on gold into a corresponding valuation for silver: the assumed relationship between the two metals was as important to the silver scenario as the gold-price assumption itself. Rather than treating silver in isolation, the calculation situated it within a precious-metals relationship in which a specified gold price and ratio jointly determined the implied result.

**2019** The discussion broadened from a forward-price scenario to an assessment of relative valuation and market sentiment. With “The ratio of 88,” the report argued that sentiment in precious metals was “currently at rock bottom.” The elevated ratio therefore served not merely as an input for projecting silver’s price, but as evidence of an unusually unfavorable relative position for silver within the precious-metals space. At this “historically extreme relative valuation,” the report stated that “The risk of price declines appears to be limited,” connecting the ratio to an assessment of downside risk.

This later treatment retained the relationship between the metals as central, while adding an important qualification: “silver remains dependent on the price movements of gold.” The ratio could signal depressed sentiment and an extreme relative valuation, but silver’s path was still tied to gold’s movements. Taken together, the two chapters show an evolution from using the ratio to calculate a silver-price outcome under a particular gold scenario to using it as a valuation and sentiment indicator that may shape the assessment of silver’s downside risk.
