#### 10 Reasons for the Commodity Bull Market 

- **Capex shortage:** After a ten-year bear market, a significant investment backlog has built up, structurally limiting production volumes. 

- **Inelastic supply:** Mining capacity cannot be expanded at the click of a mouse – it takes 15 to 20 years from discovery to the start of production. 

- **Fiscal bazooka:** Globally, the focus is shifting toward higher government spending, particularly in infrastructure and defense. 

- **Resource nationalism:** Nations are hoarding their raw materials, controlling exports, and weaponizing supply chains. The result is permanently higher geopolitical risk premiums. 

- **Institutional ignorance:** Pension funds and endowments remain structurally underallocated in commodities, often still with zero allocation. 

- **Inflation hedge:** Latent concerns about structurally higher inflation are driving capital out of bonds and into hard assets with intrinsic value.

- **1969–1980 (11-year trend):** Commodities outperformed amid stagflation, oil crises, and the end of Bretton Woods. The ratio reached its historical high in 1980 at over 1.00. 

- **1981–1999 (19-year trend):** The great disinflation and stock market bull run. Commodities fell structurally behind, and the ratio dropped by around 95%. 

- **2000–2008 (9-year trend):** The China boom, the commodity supercycle, and a weak US dollar – a strong recovery for commodities. 

- **2009–present (17-year trend):** The longest period of underperformance in recent history since the global financial crisis. The ratio has fallen to a record low of around 0.02. 

**Three observations stand out.** First, supercycles are not measured in quarters but in decades: Historically, they have lasted between 9 and 19 years. At 17 years, the current downtrend is already on the long end of that range, suggesting that the next upswing may be closer than consensus assumes. Second, the ratio shows clear signs of bottoming out. Third, commodities are more severely undervalued relative to stocks than at any time since the data series began – even the lows of 1971 and 1999 were less extreme. 

Historically, such trend reversals have rarely been gentle, but have often been accompanied by a dramatic shift – triggered by geopolitical shocks, supply bottlenecks, or a change in monetary policy. As is clear, there is no shortage of such triggers in 2026.
