# Gold valuation & price targets

How to value an asset with no cash flow, no earnings, and no yield — the
triangulation via ratio analysis, the Shadow Gold Price (monetary-base backing),
and probability-weighted scenarios. Distinct from [technical chart analysis](technical_analysis.md).

> "It is impossible to calculate a 'fair value' of gold. It is only possible to
> analyse the relative (over- or under-) valuation vis-à-vis other asset classes
> and monetary aggregates." (2012)

See also: [technical_analysis](technical_analysis.md), [gold_as_money](gold_as_money.md),
[monetary_policy_inflation](monetary_policy_inflation.md), [sovereign_debt](sovereign_debt.md).

## The core thesis

The reports open every valuation chapter with the same Austrian-School premise:
value is subjective (Menger — *"Value does not exist outside the consciousness of
men"*), gold has no cash flow or yield, so DCF/equity methods do not apply. They
formalize the thesis with **Jim Grant's formula: gold price = 1/T**, where T is
trust in the currency guardians. "1 divided by a falling number is the definition
of a bull market and of a decline in trust" (2012).

Because a single "fair value" is unattainable, the authors **triangulate** with
three complementary lenses, publishing *price targets* rather than fair-value
estimates:
- **(a) Monetary-base backing / Shadow Gold Price** — a fundamentally monetary model.
- **(b) Ratio analysis (relative valuation)** — gold vs. oil, silver, commodities,
  stocks, etc.
- **(c) Scenario analysis** — probability-weighted scenarios around the Fed balance
  sheet (introduced 2013).

## Valuation methods in detail

### (a) The Shadow Gold Price / monetary-base backing
The signature monetary model (sourced to QB Asset Management / Paul Brodsky & Lee
Quaintance). The Bretton Woods formula: **Shadow Gold Price = US monetary base ÷
US official gold reserves**. The price rises mechanically as the base expands.

The US monetary base has been "fully covered by US gold reserves twice in history"
— at the 1980 peak, gold covered **168%** of the base; coverage had fallen to
**16%** by 2012 (and ~11.5% in 2013). Implied per-ounce prices:

| Coverage level | 2012 | 2015 |
|----------------|------|------|
| 100% (full SGP) | **~$10,000** | **>$18,000** |
| 40% (Fed Act 1914) | $4,040 | $7,300 |
| 25% (1945-71 rule) | $2,525 | $4,500 |

The SGP more than doubles across the period because of QE-driven base expansion.
SGP snapshots: 1971 = $232; 1980 = $507; 2008 = $3,598; 2012 = $10,062.

**First appearance (2011).** The SGP is introduced to the corpus in the 2011
"Possible Targets" chapter with its full Bretton-Woods-lineage spelled out ("the
way the exchange rate between paper and money was calculated during the Bretton
Woods Agreement"), the 40%-coverage table (Federal Reserve Act of 1914 reference),
and the "fully covered twice in history" chart — making 2011 the technical
ground-zero for the model used in every later year. The 2011 chapter also stages
the gold-as-debt-solution argument directly: "less than 2.6% of US government debt
is covered by gold, which is clearly below the long-term median of 5%"; full
coverage would require ~$57,000/oz.

### (b) Ratio analysis (relative valuation)
Gold is always the numerator (a rising ratio = gold's relative strength). The
explicit conclusion each year: gold is undervalued vs. financial assets and
monetary aggregates. Key ratios (2013-2015): gold/silver (~61-74x vs. avg 54-56x);
gold/oil (~11-18x vs. avg 15x); gold/commodities (2.5-2.8x vs. avg 1.6x);
gold/S&P 500 (0.55x in 2015 vs. avg 1.22x). The 2014 report notes gold vs. the
*monetary base* is "actually trading at new all-time lows." In 2016 the ratios are
deepened: gold/oil (1 oz buys 30 barrels vs. avg 14), gold/copper (a "confidence
indicator"), and gold/silver reframed as a "deflation/reflation ratio."

### (c) Gold-coverage of debt & money supply
Only **2.4% of US government debt is covered by US gold reserves** (2012, vs. a
long-term average of 4.9%); reaching the 1980 high would require gold at
**~$16,000**. Money-supply ratios: gold/M0 would need $12,000 to hit its 1980
ratio; gold/M3 → $8,300.

### (d) Inflation-adjusted historical highs
The **1980 nominal peak of $850** is the anchor. Its inflation-adjusted value: the
CPI-adjusted peak = **$2,481** (2016); the ShadowStats-adjusted (alternative
inflation) peak = **~$13,177** (2016). The long-standing $2,300 target was first
set in 2008 against this anchor; its **first published derivation** appears in the
2010 chapter "Why the inflation-adjusted high of USD 2,300 is our target price,"
which walks through the official-CPI ($2,300), ShadowStats ($7,494), and MZM
(~$10,000) variants side by side, and notes the 1980 number was a "short-lived
spike" (the January 1980 average of $675 would only require $1,897 today).

## Price targets over time

| Year | Published target | Notes |
|------|------------------|-------|
| 2007 | First explicit target: **$730** (prior year's high), then $875 (1980 nominal ATH) | Origin report: targets *below* the 1980 nominal high; the inflation-adjusted gap ("160% from its highs") is introduced as context, not yet a target |
| 2008 | **$1,200** first target, $2,300 inflation-adjusted ATH as long-run anchor | The $2,300 number is *born* here, derived from the 1980 $850 CPI-adjusted to today |
| 2009 | **$1,300** first target; $2,300 reaffirmed as end-of-cycle target | "This would make our price target of USD 2,300 at the end of the cycle appear more realistic than ever" |
| 2010 | **$1,600** (12-month); $2,300 long-term reaffirmed | The $2,300 is anchored to the inflation-adjusted 1980 high; $6,000 implied if gold matched the 1970s' 24x; the **parabolic-phase framing** enters here ("we expect the gold price to reach our long-term target price of USD 2,300 at the end of the cycle") |
| 2011 | **$2,000** (12-month); $2,300 long-term; Shadow Gold Price ~$10,000 introduced | "We continue to expect the gold price to rise at least to the inflation-adjusted all-time-high of USD 2,300/ounce… Some historical comparisons suggest even higher spheres." 2011 is also where the authors first float $7,000-10,000 as the MZM/M1-coverage target |
| 2012 | **$2,300**; cites $5,000-10,000 as "realistic"; a Pareto model yields a theoretical $8,300 by spring 2015 | "Prices between USD 5,000 and 10,000 seem realistic" |
| 2013 | Probability-weighted scenario model → **$2,230** | First quantitative scenario model |
| 2015 | **$2,300, with an explicit 3-year horizon (~mid-2018)** | First time a deadline was attached |
| 2016 | **$2,300 by June 2018** reaffirmed | "Recent events are validating our views" |

The headline number is remarkably **sticky at $2,300** across 2007-2016, but the
*framing* escalates — the Shadow Gold Price ceiling rises from ~$10,000 to
>$18,000, and $5,000-10,000 is repeatedly cited as plausible in a blow-off. (For
the 2020-onward evolution to the $4,800-by-2030 and $8,900 targets, see
[technical_analysis](technical_analysis.md).)

## The revaluation argument (2014)

The "Structural over-indebtedness argues for further upward revaluation of gold"
chapter makes the **debt-revaluation** case. Debt/GDP is a misleading metric (GDP
methodology was rewritten); the authors prefer **public-debt-to-tax-revenue**
(Japan 19.4x, Spain 9.3x, USA 9.2x). Add **implicit debt** (the "sustainability
gap" — unfunded entitlements) and the true debt is far larger. The expected
"solutions": financial repression and ultimately **wealth taxes / one-off capital
levies** (citing the IMF's "Taxing Times" and a Bundesbank paper endorsing a
one-off levy in a default scenario).

The **1934 precedent** (US revaluation to $35/oz) is the intellectual scaffold for
the SGP and coverage-ratio models. The 2016 market-cap comparison drives it home:
all gold ever mined (~183,000 t) is only **3.1% of US financial assets**, vs.
**19.6% after the 1934 devaluation** and **21.8% after 1982** — implying gold
would need to rise by roughly an order of magnitude to regain a comparable share.

## Nuance

- **The no-cash-flow problem is the opening premise, not a hidden weakness.** The
  authors explicitly reject production-cost arguments: a Van Gogh's value far
  exceeds its production costs; "objective factors, such as production costs are
  entirely irrelevant for valuations" (2015).
- **They publish their own bear case.** The 2015 report dedicates a section "A gold
  bug's nightmare" — enumerating bearish scenarios (Grexit-driven dollar strength,
  forced gold sales, an RMB devaluation, a bond crash) and naming the "Great
  Moderation" (1990s-style disinflation + growth) as the "horror scenario for
  gold." Unusual intellectual honesty for a bullish house.
- **How the targets performed.** The $2,300 target (set 2007, reaffirmed through
  2016 with a mid-2018 deadline) was **not met** within the stated horizon — gold
  traded ~$1,050-1,800 across 2012-2016. The authors' response was not to cut the
  target but to *reaffirm and extend*, framing the post-2011 decline as a mid-cycle
  correction within an ongoing secular bull driven by unsolved structural
  over-indebtedness. (The target was eventually eclipsed in 2020 and dwarfed by the
  $4,800-by-2030 call — see [technical_analysis](technical_analysis.md).)
