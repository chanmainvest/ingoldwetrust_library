# Empire & the monetary order

The long-arc historical narrative — Bretton Woods → 1971 → the current fiat
"experiment" → a coming multipolar reset — and the recurring Rome/empire analogy.
The corpus's grand-historical framing.

> "Nations are not ruined by one act of violence, but gradually and in an almost
> imperceptible manner by the depreciation of their circulating currency."
> — Kopernikus (2015)

## The core thesis

Monetary history is a recurring cycle: **sound money enables civilizational rise;
currency debasement signals and accelerates decline.** The arc runs classical
gold/silver standards → Bretton Woods (1944) → 1971 Nixon shock → the current fiat
"experiment" → a coming multipolar reset likely involving gold re-monetization.

**The Bismarck-Ruhland meta-thesis (2015).** The 2015 chapter leans on a study
commissioned by Bismarck and executed by economist Gustav Ruhland, which compared
the decline of the Greeks, Romans, Spanish, and British empires and concluded the
cause was "in all cases rooted in monetary policy." Ruhland's sequence of Roman
decline: growing inequality (Cantillon effect) → decadence → rising taxes →
welfare statism ("bread and circuses") → external military conflicts → bankers
take over government → "monetary socialism" → devaluation of coinage → price
inflation → uprisings/civil wars → chronic insolvency → barter replaces the
monetary economy. "Parallels to the present are hardly deniable."

## The Rome analogy (2022)

The most detailed Rome-to-US mapping in the corpus. Rome is presented as a proto-
laissez-faire economy (farmers protected by civil law; a credit-driven boom ended
in a 33 AD financial panic — "inevitably"). The decline mechanism:

- **Debasement.** "Every new ruler minted new coins," progressively lowering silver
  content while retaining face value. By the 3rd-century crisis, Roman coins had a
  silver content of **less than half a percent**; some from Claudius II Gothicus
  (268-270 AD) had **0.02%**. Egyptian wheat rose from 7-8 drachmae (2nd c.) to
  **120,000 drachmae** (3rd c.).
- **Diocletian as authoritarian endpoint.** His "solutions" — in-kind taxation,
  fixed prices, binding workers hereditarily to occupations, the 301 AD *Edictum de
  Pretiis* (price controls with the death penalty) — "set the European economy back
  a millennium." (Occupational freezes are "the reason we have occupational
  surnames": Zimmermann, Schumacher, Müller.)
- **Welfare-state spiral.** The Gracchi grain dole (124 BC) grew to 320,000 daily
  recipients under Augustus. Inflation → fewer productive farmers → more dole
  recipients → more inflation.
- **Industrial collapse.** Lead-emission ice cores show "Roman manufacturing
  output declined by ~80% from peak to trough." Rome's population fell from ~1.5
  million at zenith to ~70,000 by 700 AD; literacy from ~10% to ~1%.

**The US mapping.** "The Roman experience looks eerily similar to the present US
economic situation." Specific parallels: US federal tax revenue covered ~90% of
outlays (mid-20th c.) → <60% (GFC) → <50% (COVID); combined US military + social
spending now exceeds 100% of all tax receipts; in 2020, "more than two-thirds of
the ~50% bond issuance needed was monetized by the Federal Reserve." **The
Adrianople moment**: Rome's 378 AD defeat is mapped onto the US withdrawal from
Afghanistan, and onto Britain's loss of HMS Prince of Wales / Repulse (Dec 10,
1941), "which marked the end of Britain's Empire." Where are we? Past the
debasement phase, at the Adrianople-equivalent, heading toward the "Dominate"
stage. "A Dominate will be put in charge."

## The acceleration thesis (2019)

Integrates sociologist Hartmut Rosa's theory of modernity with the Austrian
monetary critique — the corpus's most theoretical framing. Three interlocking
accelerations (technical, social, pace of life) form a self-reinforcing "circle of
acceleration." The monetary dimension is the authors' specific contribution:

> "The introduction of a pure fiat money system with the closing of the gold
> window in 1971 and the recession-prevention-via-money-printing policy since the
> Greenspan era represent the monetary dimension of the epochal fault line
> separating classical modernity from late modernity."

A gold standard "prevents acceleration" because global gold supply could grow only
at small single-digit rates; pure fiat institutionalizes money-supply expansion as
"dynamic stabilization" — its structure can only be maintained by growth/
acceleration. "Total US debt/GDP requires USD 3.8 to finance USD 1 of real GDP."

## Sustainable wealth in an unsustainable system (2017)

The most prescriptive chapter — framing personal investment against a system the
authors believe is mathematically doomed. The "zero interest rate trap": two
mutually exclusive outcomes (continue low rates → distortions compound; exit low
rates → inevitable new crisis). "Monetary policy and the entire financial system
are caught in a zero interest rate trap." Hold assets that survive currency
debasement — gold central among them — because the Fed "will feel compelled to
maintain the monetary tailwind."

## Synthesis

Across 2015 → 2017 → 2019 → 2022 the thesis hardens from prediction to observation:
- **2015:** the dollar order "is slowly but surely coming to an end"; a multipolar
  system is forming via China, Russia, BRICS, AIIB, SDRs.
- **2017:** the fiat system is structurally caught in a "zero interest rate trap."
- **2019:** this is the *monetary dimension of the epochal break into late
  modernity* — acceleration itself, with credit-money as its engine.
- **2022:** the Roman template is now active — the US has passed its debasement
  phase, suffered its "Adrianople" (Afghanistan), and approaches the "Dominate."

The unifying claim, traceable through all four chapters: **gold re-monetization is
the historically consistent endgame of every fiat reserve-currency unwind**, and
the institutional scaffolding for the next order (BRICS+, SCO, gold repatriation,
de-dollarization) is already being built.
