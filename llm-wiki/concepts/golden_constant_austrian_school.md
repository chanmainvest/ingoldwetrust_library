# Jastram's Golden Constant & the Austrian School

Two linked intellectual pillars of the corpus: (1) Roy Jastram's statistical proof
that gold maintains purchasing power over centuries — but not in the short run —
and the "inflationary deflation" concept; (2) the Austrian School of Economics as
the corpus's theoretical backbone (money creation, the Cantillon effect, deflation
as cure not disease).

> "Gold is the only financial asset which outperforms in either inflation and
> deflation." (2024, on Jastram)

## Part 1 — Jastram's Golden Constant (2024)

**Who Jastram was.** Roy Jastram (1915-1991), UC Berkeley professor of business
administration. His *The Golden Constant* (1977, updated 2009), subtitled "The
English and American Experience 1560-1976," began as a 1936 Stanford paper
answering "Was money worth more in past centuries than it is today?"

**Core finding.** *The Golden Constant* provides "the first statistical proof that
gold maintained its purchasing power over very long periods — in this case more
than five centuries." The purchasing-power index shows gold continually reverting
to an "equilibrium level" after decades-long overshoots to both the upside and
downside. "Gold's purchasing power in the middle of the twentieth century was very
nearly the same as in the midst of the seventeenth century."

**Critical nuance — gold is NOT a short-run inflation hedge.** "Gold can be a
relatively poor hedge against inflation, at least in the early stages." Gold hedged
poorly during WWI but well during the Napoleonic Wars, the Civil War, and WWII.
Jastram attributed the early-stage underperformance to New World gold imports that
expanded money supply and temporarily lowered gold's purchasing power.

**The deflation surprise — "inflationary deflation."** Jastram's "most surprising"
conclusion: "the gold price does very well during periods of deflation." Gold is
the only financial asset which outperforms in either inflation and deflation. In
deflation, money's real value rises; since "Gold is money. Everything else is
credit" (J.P. Morgan), gold rises against goods and against credit-supported
assets.

**The long-term debt-cycle evidence.** In the winter phases of the three prior
Kondratieff cycles, gold rose in real terms while equities crashed (2024):

| K-cycle | Equities | Gold nominal | Gold real |
|---------|----------|--------------|-----------|
| K-1 (1825-1843) | −78% | 0% | **+26%** |
| K-2 (1873-1896) | −22% | −10% | **+20%** |
| K-3 (1929-1933) | −89% | +69% | **+122%** |

**"Inflationary deflation" defined.** A paradox where, depending on whether you
measure in gold or fiat, you see simultaneous inflation and deflation. Incrementum's
thesis: policy makers will fight debt deflation with unlimited credit creation
("whatever it takes redux"), so the resolution is "debt reduction via inflation;
and deflation — in the price of almost everything, as measured in 'real money,'
i.e. gold." Gold wins in either the deflationary or the inflationary endgame.

## Part 2 — The Austrian School as framework

**Money's origin (2010).** Drawing on Mises's *Theory of Money and Credit*, money
emerges from indirect barter as the "most marketable means of exchange." Over
centuries the choice narrowed to gold and silver due to "durability, divisibility,
and of course the limited supply." Money is "covered by goods" — to acquire it, one
must provide a service the market demands.

**Money supply — AMS (2009).** The Austrian Money Supply (AMS) is defined narrowly:
cash + demand + savings deposits only. AMS growth "recorded a considerable increase
both in USD and euro since autumn 2008." USD AMS substantially outgrew euro AMS
during 2000-2005; euro-zone growth "remained constant above 10% over a longer
period." Gold and oil prices tracked AMS growth closely.

**The Austrian distinction: monetary inflation (cause) vs. price inflation (effect).**
This is the framework underpinning the corpus. Quoted from the *Federal Reserve
Bulletin, 1919*: "Inflation is the process of making addition to currencies not
based on a commensurate increase in the production of goods." Price inflation is
the downstream symptom; the cause is monetary expansion.

## The Cantillon effect (2013)

Richard Cantillon was "the first person to recognize" that newly created money is
"neither evenly nor simultaneously distributed." Early receivers benefit; later
receivers suffer. "A wealth transfer from later users to earlier users takes place."
Monetary expansion is therefore "never neutral." Hayek's honey metaphor: it spreads
slowly from the middle to the periphery.

**Asset-price inflation first, then CPI.** Prices "will tend to increase, but at
different rates and speeds." The single-number CPI is a fallacy. The reserve-
currency issuer (the dollar) "benefits the most from inflationary policy and is the
biggest beneficiary of the Cantillon effect."

**Wealth-redistribution evidence.** In the US, 1979-2011: average household income
+64%; top 1% **+300%**; lowest quintile only **+18%**. The GINI coefficient "has
reached historical extremes"; in the US it is "at the same level as in the 1920s
prior to the Great Depression."

## Deflation from the Austrian perspective (2014)

**Falling prices are natural/benign.** "The majority of consumers are happy with
falling prices. This has been evident for years with respect to technology, such as
smart phones or television sets, or long-distance travel." Austrian definition:
deflation is "an increase in money's quality and a decrease in its quantity."

**No empirical deflation-depression link.** The Fed Minneapolis study (Atkeson &
Kehoe, 2004, 17 countries over 100 years): "nearly 90% of the episodes with
deflation did not have depression… beyond the Great Depression, the notion that
deflation and depression are linked virtually disappears."

**Historical frequency.** Since 1913 (Fed founding) deflation essentially
vanished: only **12% of years** saw annual price deflation, versus **43% of all
years** before the Fed.

**Why central banks fight it so hard — the debt angle.** "Due to high debts — and
only due to high debts — falling prices become a societal and political ordeal"
(Hülsmann). In a leveraged fractional-reserve system, falling prices raise the real
value of debt; "permanent expansion of the money and credit supply thus becomes an
end in itself for the debt money system." Arthur Salz: "Scratch an inflationist and
you will find a debtor under the skin."

**CPI by monetary regime:** gold/silver convertibility +0.15%/yr; gold-exchange
standard +2.51%/yr; fiat +4.07%/yr. "The more consistent the gold backing, the
lower long-term inflation and the higher real economic growth will be."

## Notable data & quotes

- **Jastram:** "the first statistical proof that gold maintained its purchasing
  power over very long periods — more than five centuries" (2024).
- **J.P. Morgan:** "Gold is money. Everything else is credit" (2024).
- **Federal Reserve Bulletin (1919, cited 2013):** "Inflation is the process of
  making addition to currencies not based on a commensurate increase in the
  production of goods."
- **Cantillon redistribution (2013):** top 1% income +300% vs lowest quintile +18%
  (US, 1979-2011).
- **Deflation frequency (2014):** 43% of years pre-1913 vs 12% post-1913.
- **Baader (2014):** "Falling prices are not the cause of economic and financial
  crises, but their consequence — and at the same time their cure."
- **Hülsmann (2014):** the harmfulness of price deflation is "one of the sanctified
  dogmas of today's monetary policy."
- **Gold-standard stability data (precursor, 2009):** the 1879–1914 vs post-1971
  comparison (0.2% avg at 2.2% volatility vs 4.6% at 2.8%) — later a Jastram-
  adjacent staple — first appears in *Is Gold Pricing in the Coming Inflation?*
  alongside the gold-inflation correlation (0.48 monthly 1971–2009; 0.76 in the
  1978–1982 high-inflation window). The Roman-denarius decline (97% → 2% silver
  over 300 years) appears in the same year's *A New Gold Standard?* as the
  historical Exhibit A for "currency debasement = societal decay."
