# Gold storage & custody

Physical gold storage — the recurring "fact-checking jurisdictions" series, why
physical location matters, counterparty risk in allocated vs unallocated
accounts, and the companion thread of historical gold confiscations.

> "Unlike fiat currencies or other financial assets, physical gold is no one's
> liability and is independent of the functioning of financial institutions."
> (2020)

See also: [war_on_cash_cbdc](war_on_cash_cbdc.md),
[manipulation_intervention](manipulation_intervention.md),
[central_bank_gold](central_bank_gold.md).

## The core thesis

Because gold is the one asset that is no one's liability, the choice of *where*
it is stored is where counterparty and sovereign risk actually crystallize. The
recurring series (written with Matterhorn Asset Management / GoldSwitzerland)
evaluates jurisdictions on security, trust, rule of law, and absence of a
confiscation tradition — advocating allocated, segregated physical storage
outside the banking system, diversified across several jurisdictions.

The criteria (stated 2021/2022): economic freedom, rule of law and enforcement of
private property rights, political/economic stability, historical track record —
layered with tax treatment (VAT exemption), anonymity/privacy, and geographic
remoteness.

> "No single storage solution or country should be considered perfect…
> diversification amongst storage locations should also be taken into
> consideration." (2020)

## The fact-checking series

- **2019 — Liechtenstein, Switzerland, Singapore.** Established the template and
  the three "classic" safe-haven jurisdictions.
- **2020 — New Zealand, Australia, Dubai.** Added common-law jurisdictions with
  geographic remoteness (NZ "safety at the end of the world") plus an emerging
  Middle East hub. Introduced the Human Freedom Index and Transparency
  International as scoring metrics. First to flag a prior confiscation
  (Australia 1959 Banking Act).
- **2021 — Austria, USA, Cayman Islands.** An "insider tip" (Austria, for bank
  secrecy + the Philharmonic coin), the superpower (USA — "middling" as a
  custodian, weighed down by the 1933 confiscation), and a pure offshore tax
  haven (Caymans — no taxes at all). Companion chapter: "A Brief History of Gold
  Confiscations."
- **2022 — Germany, Canada, UK + Channel Islands.** Germany (strong rule of law,
  but EU AML pressure and a looming "European Asset Registry"); Canada (spooked
  by the Trudeau Emergencies Act / trucker-convoy asset freezes, and notable for
  having **emptied its central-bank gold vaults in 2016**); UK/London (the world's
  largest physical vault — BoE ~5,800 t, LBMA vaults 9,636 t, $60 bn/day traded).

## Key sub-arguments / criteria

1. **Rule of law / property rights.** Foundational, every year (NZ #1 Human
   Freedom Index; Germany WJP Rule of Law #5). But the 2022 Canada chapter and
   the 2021 confiscation chapter both stress rule of law is **suspendable in
   "emergencies."**
2. **Allocated/segregated physical vs. bank-held/unallocated.** Preference for
   private segregated vaults over banks (Australia's bail-in exemption, 2020).
   The 2021 chapter sharpens this: gold ETFs and futures are the *most likely*
   intervention target, not physical metal.
3. **Confiscation risk & the 1933 precedent.** USA 1933 (Executive Order 6102;
   compensation $20.67, revalued to $35 in 1934; ~30% compliance). Pattern: "When
   a government finds itself in a monetary crisis, it attempts to confiscate
   private wealth" (2021). Mitigant: today gold is only ~1% of global wealth vs.
   ~30% of US wealth in 1933.
4. **Tax treatment (VAT exemption).** A hard differentiator: gold VAT-exempt in
   Austria, Germany, NZ, Australia, Dubai; no taxes at all in the Caymans and
   Channel Islands. Silver/platinum/palladium *do* attract VAT in Austria/Germany.
5. **Anonymity / privacy erosion.** Germany cut the anonymous cash-purchase
   ceiling from €14,999.99 (2017) to **€1,999.99 (2020)**. The EU's planned
   European Asset Registry (which would list gold) is flagged as the next step.
6. **Liquidity / market depth.** UK/London is the apex ($60 bn/day; 9,636 t in
   LBMA vaults). This favors UK/USA for investors who need liquidity, even though
   they rank lower on pure "safety."

## Named confiscation events (2016, 2021)

- **USA 1933** — Executive Order 6102 (April 5, 1933); ~30% compliance; $10,000
  fine or 10 years jail. Eisenhower (1961) extended the ban to gold held abroad;
  Ford repealed in 1974.
- **Australia 1959** — Banking Act seized private gold for paper money;
  suspended 1976.
- **UK 1966** — ownership limited to four coins; rescinded 1971.
- **Italy 1935** — Mussolini's "Gold for the Fatherland"; 250,000 wedding rings
  in Rome, 35 tonnes.
- **Nazi Germany 1938-44** — ~600 tonnes stolen (Austria's central bank 78 t in
  1938).
- **South Korea 1977** — "Gold Collection Campaign"; 230 tonnes donated.
- **India 2015-** — Modi Gold Monetization Scheme; only 13 t collected against
  ~25,000 t private stock.

## Nuance — the internal tension

The house leans offshore/pro-diversification (the firm partners with an allocated
offshore custodian), but there is a genuine, acknowledged tension:

- **The confiscation rhetoric vs. the confiscation conclusion.** The storage
  chapters and 2016 ban chapter lean on confiscation fear, yet the **2021
  confiscation chapter explicitly concludes the opposite** — "the risk of physical
  gold confiscation is low by historical standards," "gold is no longer the
  primary target of theft," "the balance of reward vs. risk has never been
  greater." The series uses confiscation history as a *scoring criterion* without
  claiming high base-rate risk today.
- **Proximity vs. offshore.** The reports repeatedly validate domestic/proximate
  storage for practical access (UAE for residents, USA for Americans). The house
  view is not doctrinaire offshore-only.
- **The modern threat model shifted.** The real urgency is not literal seizure
  but **financial repression via surveillance and capital controls**: anonymity
  ceilings falling, the EU Asset Registry, Canada's Emergencies Act digital-asset
  freezes, the Western confiscation of Russian FX reserves as a "slippery slope."

> Net: own allocated physical gold, store a meaningful portion offshore in
> rule-of-law jurisdictions with VAT exemption and no confiscation tradition,
> diversify across several, and keep some close to home for liquidity.
