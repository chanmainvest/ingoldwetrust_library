# Gold's image problem & the psychology of underallocation

Why gold is so emotionally charged, dismissed by mainstream finance, and
underallocated — the cognitive biases (normalcy bias, cognitive dissonance),
"aurophobia," career risk, and the "barbarous relic" Keynesian inheritance.

> Gold has compounded at 9.4% p.a. in USD since 1971, carries no counterparty
> risk, is weakly correlated to other assets, and is geopolitically neutral — yet
> 72% of family offices hold 0% gold. (2026)

See also: [portfolio_diversification](portfolio_diversification.md),
[status_quo_framework](status_quo_framework.md).

## The core thesis

Gold's marginalization in Western finance is **not primarily a rational, data-
driven verdict but a psychologically and institutionally entrenched heuristic.**
The 2026 chapter calls this the "gold paradox": the empirical case is already
sufficient; the deficit is psychological and institutional, not informational. The
recurring biases invoked:

- **Cognitive dissonance** (2012): people reject information that makes them look
  "stupid, immoral, or irrational." The first gold purchase costs "an enormous
  mental effort" (2013).
- **Normalcy bias** (2012): "what has been that way, will remain that way." After
  decades of disinflation and reliable stock-bond diversification, investors treat
  historically contingent conditions as the natural state.
- **Status quo / recency bias** (2026): portfolio behavior adapts more slowly than
  macro regimes; the 60/40 framework persists because it worked for ~40 years.
- **Mental accounting** (2013): "gold pays no interest" looms large because
  dividends are booked as permanent income while capital gains are not.

## The "aurophobia" concept (2012, 2013)

The term — "this pathological fear of, or aggression towards, gold" — was
introduced in 2012 and elaborated in 2013. The core claim: this aversion "does not
seem to exist for any other currency or other asset class… we have not heard of
such a profound aversion against copper, we do not know of any 'bond haters'." The
2013 chapter names four mechanisms: mental accounting, normalcy bias, cognitive
dissonance, and the availability heuristic.

**Rebutting the famous skeptic.** Warren Buffett's anti-gold case is dismissed as
"comparing apples and oranges" — gold should be compared to *cash*, not equities,
because it is "an alternative currency and not an investment." The same arguments
(no intrinsic value, no industrial use, no interest) apply to the US dollar. The
2012 chapter adds a psychobiographical gloss: Buffett's aversion may be
"rebellious behaviour against his father," Senator Howard Buffett, a gold-standard
advocate. Munger: "civilised people do not buy gold" — rebutted with the point
that this effectively calls billions in India and China "uncivilised."

## How the diagnosis evolved

The diagnosis has **deepened and institutionalized but not fundamentally changed**;
the framing layer shifts:

- **2012 (Erste Group)** — Psychological/diagnostic. Cognitive dissonance, normalcy
  bias, confirmation bias, the aurophobia label. Tone is clinical ("pathological
  fear"). Focus on the individual investor's mind.
- **2013 (Incrementum)** — Systematizes the behavioral-economics list (mental
  accounting, availability heuristic added).
- **2024 ("Image Problem in the West")** — Moves from individual psychology to
  **sociology and narrative warfare**. Ben Hunt's "Narrative Machine," the Overton
  window, mass formation (Desmet). Gold's bad image is partly attributed to a
  *deliberate defamation campaign* by those with a vested interest in
  discouraging gold as a fiat alternative.
- **2026 ("Psychology of Underallocation")** — The most analytically rigorous and
  least polemical. Recasts the question as **bounded rationality and ecological
  heuristics** (Simon, Gigerenzer, Kahneman System 1/2). The 60/40 is treated as a
  *successful heuristic that has outlived its environment*. Notably **less
  accusatory** than 2012 — "investors did not 'make a mistake'; they adopted a
  heuristic well suited to a specific and unusually persistent macroeconomic
  environment."

## Key mechanisms

1. **Status quo / normalcy bias.** The dominant mechanism. The post-1980
   disinflationary regime is treated as the default.
2. **The "barbarous relic" Keynesian inheritance** — quoted explicitly in 2012 and
   2026. The reputation traced to the 1980s-90s bear market.
3. **Recency / availability bias.** Gold's poor 2013-2019 performance; the 2026
   correction seized as "confirmation that gold is inherently speculative."
4. **Career and reputational risk.** Keynes: "better for reputation to fail
   conventionally than to succeed unconventionally." Grantham: deviating requires
   accepting "a big dose of career and business risk." Conformity is "professional
   insurance."
5. **Benchmarking and the EMH.** The efficient-market hypothesis "reduces the
   perceived benefit of deviating materially from prevailing consensus
   allocations," fostering "behavioral inertia and a degree of herd behavior"
   (2026).
6. **Narrative Machine / Overton window (2024).** The defamation of gold via media
   ("Gold Fools," "Nazi narrative") and regulatory creep (US $10,000 Form 8300, EU
   €10,000 cash limit, Germany's €2,000 anonymous-purchase ceiling).

## Nuance

**Structural diagnosis, with an emergent thaw.** The 2024 chapter is the most
fixed in seeing the image problem as deliberate and structural. The 2026 chapter
is more open-ended: heuristics change only when (a) the old one repeatedly fails,
(b) failure is attributed to the heuristic not luck, (c) alternatives become
socially/professionally legitimized, and (d) a simple alternative rule is
available. It cites "early signals" (ETF inflows positive since Q2 2024; Mike
Wilson's 60/20/20; Dalio and Gundlach endorsing gold) but "no mass reallocation…
has occurred so far." The rally alone will not fix it — career risk and
benchmarking must shift first. As 2024 puts it, paraphrasing Mikhailovich: "no one
cares until everyone will."

**Counter-argument acknowledged.** The 2026 chapter is notably concessive: the
60/40 neglect of gold is called a "rational response" to the Great Moderation.
Heuristics "are not good or bad, rational or irrational per se, but only relative
to an environment" (Gigerenzer). Gold's volatility is acknowledged, and the 2026
correction is treated as a real psychological event, not dismissed. The shift from
2012 to 2026 is a softening from psychological diagnosis toward structural-
institutional analysis, while retaining the core claim that the environment has
changed and heuristics have not yet caught up.
