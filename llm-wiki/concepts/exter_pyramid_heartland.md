# Exter's Pyramid, Plaza 2.0 & the Heartland

The deflationary counter-narrative — three linked chapters that form a coherent arc
distinct from the corpus's dominant inflation thesis: (1) Exter's Inverted Pyramid
(the deflationary-collapse mechanism, gold as the outside asset), (2) the Plaza
Accord 2.0 historical precedent for coordinated currency realignment, and (3)
Mackinder's Heartland Theory applied to the monetary order.

> "The U.S. and world economies are on the threshold of a deflationary crash that
> will make the 1930s look like a boom. Gold will be the single best investment to
> own." — John Exter (2019)

## 1. Exter's Inverted Pyramid (2019)

**Who John Exter was.** Harvard-trained economist (1910-2006): Fed Board economist,
founder-governor of the Central Bank of Ceylon, division chief for the Middle East
at the IBRD, and from 1954 vice president of the NY Fed in charge of international
banking and precious metals. Described as "arguably the only honest central banker
history has seen." Close personal friend of Mises, Hayek, Rothbard, and Jacques
Rueff.

**What the pyramid shows.** An *inverted* (upside-down) debt pyramid: derivatives
and speculative assets at the broad top, then corporate bonds / EM debt, stocks,
government bonds, and cash & Treasuries, narrowing to gold at the bottom tip. The
critical nuance: **gold is NOT inside the pyramid.** Every layer inside is debt —
even cash is a central-bank liability. Gold is nobody's liability, so it is
"outside" the debt pyramid; it is the only exit.

**The deflationary-collapse thesis.** As confidence fails, liquidity drains from
the top (illiquid derivatives) and flows downward. When confidence fails even in
Treasuries and fiat cash, investors must leave the pyramid entirely and enter gold.
Exter believed a fiat system "inevitably ends in tragedy" and "a deflationary
depression that could continue for several generations." The 2008 crisis is framed
as a near-miss "Exter moment" — a deflationary death spiral averted only by massive
central-bank intervention that "postponed the inevitable."

**The Exter-vs-Mises tension.** This is the chapter's central intellectual
conflict. Mises and many Austrians warned that constant money-supply expansion
must end in (hyper)inflation. Exter believed the more likely endgame was
*deflation* — the bursting of an ever-larger credit pyramid. They were close
personal friends, and "Mises never agreed with Exter's view that a deflationary
collapse is more likely." The authors resolve the tension by arguing *both* are
right simultaneously: inflation of credit has occurred inside the shadow banking
system (financial assets), not consumer goods; the credit bubble's collapse would
be deflationary. "Combining both approaches better reflects the confused state of
our current financial system."

## 2. The Plaza Accord 2.0 argument (2019)

**Historical precedent.** The Plaza Accord (22 September 1985) — G5 finance
ministers meeting at the Plaza Hotel, NYC — agreed to coordinate FX intervention
to weaken the overvalued USD. The Louvre Accord (22 February 1987) then tried to
*stabilize* the dollar after it had fallen too far.

- The real trade-weighted Dollar Index rose ~50% (87.7 in July 1980 → 131.6 in
  March 1985). The US current account went from a small 1980/81 surplus to a
  deficit of 2.9% of GDP by 1985.
- **Devaluation:** short-term target was a 10-12% dollar devaluation. One week
  post-Plaza: yen +11.8%, DEM and FRF +7.8% each. After Louvre: yen +17%, GBP
  +15.5%.

**The role of gold.** The 1980s were poor for gold overall, but: "After the Plaza
Accord in 1985, gold managed a trend reversal in USD terms, and in the wake of the
Louvre Accord the new uptrend continued until the end of 1987. Gold once again
confirmed its status as the antagonist of the US dollar."

**Plaza 2.0 for today (written 2019).** Proponents point to the dollar's ~40%
appreciation 2011-2016, persistent US current-account deficits, and Germany/Japan/
China surpluses. The Big Mac Index (Jan 2019) implied undervaluation: renminbi
~−45%, yen ~−35.5%, euro ~−17%. Options: multilateral deal, G20 agreement,
bilateral US-China deal, or unilateral intervention. Big obstacles: a 2013 G8
agreement to refrain from FX intervention (an "Anti-Plaza Accord"); daily FX volume
has risen 10× since 1985 to >$5 trn, making intervention expensive. The authors are
skeptical of a true Plaza 2.0 but warn the reset is coming "whether we like it or
not."

## 3. Mackinder's Heartland Theory applied to money (2026)

**The geopolitical theory.** Sir Halford Mackinder's 1904 "Geographical Pivot of
History" — "the founding moment of geopolitics." Global power is anchored in
geography: the prize is control of the Eurasian landmass (the "World Island"). His
1919 dictum: *"Who rules East Europe commands the Heartland; who rules the Heartland
commands the World Island; who rules the World Island commands the world."* It is a
theory of land power vs. sea power.

**The monetary analogue (the authors' innovation):**
- **Gold = the monetary Heartland.** Like Mackinder's geographic Heartland, gold is
  "impregnable": neutral, geopolitically unaligned, supply-constrained, nobody's
  liability, high stock-to-flow. "Gold is the impregnable monetary Heartland — the
  ultimate expression of 'land power.'"
- **The US dollar system = maritime/offshore power projection.** Eurodollars are
  "oceans of dollar liquidity" encircling Eurasia; SWIFT, Treasuries as collateral,
  Fed swap lines, and dollar-denominated commodity trade all act as *containment
  tools* — "just as a navy controls the seas."
- **Paper gold markets (LBMA, COMEX) = the monetary Rimland** — the buffer zone
  where land and sea power clash. >95% of LBMA trades settle in unallocated (paper)
  accounts.

**The gold price as real-time indicator.** "The gold price in US dollars is a
real-time expression of Mackinder's geopolitical conflict in the monetary sphere."
A rising gold price signals dollar debasement, the BRICS-led structural challenge,
and breakdown of paper-gold containment. SWIFT removal of Russian banks (March
2022) and the freezing of ~$300 bn of Russian reserves was the catalyst for
central-bank gold accumulation. In 2024, gold exceeded US Treasuries as a share of
international reserves for the first time since the mid-1990s.

## 4. The deflationary loss-of-confidence thesis (2011, 2012)

**"Is a gold standard deflationary per se?" (2011).** No. A study (Rolnick & Weber,
1997) of 15 nations 1820-1994 found average inflation of **9.17% under fiat vs.
1.75% under gold/bimetallic standards**. "The stronger the gold pegging, the lower
inflation." Deflation is reframed as a "healthy shakeout." During the Great
Depression, nations that *left* the gold standard triggered latent gold demand;
those that stayed saw gold appreciate (1929-1934: up to ~70% in Czechoslovakia,
64% in UK/US). "It was not the gold standard that failed, but those to whose care
it had been entrusted" (Rittershausen).

**"Gold in an environment of a deflationary loss of confidence" (2012).** Gold is
mischaracterized as *only* an inflation hedge. In the *first (deflationary) decile*
of CPI performance since 1970, gold recorded *positive* performance. Historically
(Jastram), gold's purchasing power rose during deflationary phases: 1864-1897
(+89% real), 1929-1933 (+44% real). Mechanism (Exter restated): "In a deflationary
depression, as in an hourglass, liquidity flows from the higher part of the pyramid
downwards." Gold is "cash of the highest quality."

## Synthesis

The three themes form a coherent arc *distinct from the corpus's dominant
inflation/debasement narrative*. Exter (with roots in the 2011/2012 deflation
chapters) provides the *mechanism*: the debt pyramid implodes inward in a
deflationary loss of confidence; gold is the outside asset you flee to. The Plaza
chapter supplies the *historical playbook* for coordinated currency realignment
and gold's response. The 2026 Mackinder chapter provides the *grand-strategic
frame*: the monetary crisis is the visible expression of a century-old contest —
maritime dollar power (paper gold, SWIFT, eurodollars) versus the land-power
Heartland (physical gold, BRICS, resources) — with the gold price as the real-time
scoreboard.
