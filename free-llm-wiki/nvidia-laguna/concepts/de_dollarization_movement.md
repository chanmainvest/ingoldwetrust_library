# De-dollarization Movement

The de-dollarization movement represents a systemic shift away from the US dollar's dominance as the global reserve and trade currency, driven by major economies seeking monetary multipolarity and reduced exposure to American financial hegemony. This trend reflects growing dissatisfaction with dollar-centric systems among key nations and carries profound implications for global financial architecture, commodity pricing, and the role of gold as an alternative monetary anchor. The movement has evolved from theoretical discussions to concrete policy actions across multiple sectors, signaling a long-term realignment of international economic relationships.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** marked the initial recognition of de-dollarization as an accelerating process, with the report noting that "statements of Trump, Pence, Greenspan and Zoellick are compatible with the above discussed process of de-dollarization." The analysis highlighted "steps toward a multi-polar future" and observed that "de-dollarization are in the meantime coming thick and fast," suggesting that the transition away from dollar dominance was gaining tangible momentum through high-level political discourse.

By **2018**, the focus shifted to concrete actions by countries moving away from using the US dollar as the primary reserve and trade currency. The report emphasized that "many of the world's countries... no longer respect the US" and acknowledged that "the process of de-dollarization is likely to take decades," framing it as a structural transformation rather than a temporary trend.

**2020** brought a sense of urgency with the declaration that "the gradual de-dollarization of the world" had reached a critical phase where "Russia and China have presented the USA with a difficult choice." The analysis noted that "Many countries in the world began turning away from using the dollar as a reserve currency," indicating that major economies were actively reducing dollar dependence through gold purchases, alternative currencies, and non-dollar trade arrangements.

In **2021**, the report expanded the scope to include Europe's growing dissatisfaction, highlighting "dissatisfaction of Europe, Russia, China and Iran with having to trade oil in US dollars." European policymakers were quoted as saying "We need to reduce sanctions risks by bolstering our technological independence, by switching to payments in our national currencies," revealing that the push for alternatives was driven by both economic and geopolitical considerations.

**2023** saw the movement mature into what the report termed "the resistance of countries like China, Russia, the rest of the BRICS" and noted that "In 2023, we have moved from rather passive preparations to active reorientation of the East – led by China." This represented a shift from defensive measures to proactive restructuring of financial systems, with the report identifying "the resistance of countries like China, Russia, the rest of the BRICS, parts of Europe and the Middle East to the dominant position of the US dollar."

**2024** brought new participants into the de-dollarization effort, with the report observing that "last year we saw significant progress by players such as China and Russia to undermine the greenback's dominance" and noting that "Crucial players like Saudi Arabia and the UAE have joined the fray." The analysis concluded that "The rallying cry to escape the dollar system is spreading to new ears," indicating that the movement was gaining broader international legitimacy beyond the initial Eastern powers.
