# Austrian Business Cycle Theory

The Austrian Business Cycle Theory (ABCT), pioneered by Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich August von Hayek, explains how central bank interventions—particularly excessive monetary liquidity—create artificial economic booms that inevitably culminate in painful corrections. This framework provides a critical lens for understanding the relationship between monetary policy, asset price dynamics, and inflation, making it a cornerstone concept for analyzing financial market distortions.

## How the argument evolved

**2014** established the foundational mechanism of ABCT by documenting the temporal sequence of price increases: "prices of capital goods (= asset price inflation) are the first to increase" while "consumer price inflation only sets in at a later stage." This early treatment emphasized that asset price inflation acts as the initial signal of monetary disruption, with capital goods serving as the canaries in the coal mine for broader economic instability.

**2016** then connected this theoretical framework directly to empirical evidence from the pre-2008 period, identifying that "the disproportionate growth rate of M3 in the time period before 2008 was the root cause of various bubbles and misallocations of capital." The report explicitly labeled this pattern as "a textbook example of Austrian business cycle theory (ABCT)," demonstrating how excessive monetary expansion prior to the financial crisis created the conditions for the subsequent crisis.

In **2020**, the analysis returned to the core temporal dynamic, observing that "The present development corresponds exactly to the textbook description of the ABCT" as "asset prices rise first (= asset price inflation) and only then does consumer price inflation (= inflation) set in." This reinforced the theory's predictive power in describing contemporary monetary developments.

**2023** provided the conceptual synthesis, explaining that ABCT "pioneered by Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich August von Hayek" describes how "flooding the markets with liquidity triggers an artificial boom" that must eventually correct. This modern framing positioned ABCT as an essential tool for understanding why today's monetary policies, despite their intentions, may sow the seeds of future economic disruption.
