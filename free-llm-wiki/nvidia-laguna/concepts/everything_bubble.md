# Everything Bubble

The "Everything Bubble" is a concept describing the pervasive overvaluation of financial assets across all major classes—stocks, bonds, and real estate—driven by unprecedented monetary stimulus and money supply inflation. This phenomenon challenges traditional investment wisdom and raises fundamental questions about the sustainability of the current economic model. The term captures the idea that no asset class is immune to bubble-like conditions, creating systemic risk and questioning the viability of classic portfolio strategies.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** marked the initial articulation of this concept in the report's introduction, where the authors explicitly stated that "stocks, bonds and real estate have all become as overvalued as we have ever seen." The analysis positioned this overvaluation as a direct consequence of money supply inflation, suggesting that asset prices across the board had detached from fundamental valuations. The report questioned the "classic investment portfolio" paradigm, arguing that such a diversified approach "must be critically questioned" in an environment where every major asset class exhibited bubble-like characteristics. The term "Everything Bubble" was introduced to describe this universal overvaluation phenomenon.

**2019** saw the concept refined and contextualized within a broader narrative about monetary policy and economic growth models. The report adopted Jesse Felder's term "The Everything Bubble" to describe the current environment, emphasizing that this bubble was sustained by "excessive monetary stimulus" and a "misplaced trust in the growth model." The analysis highlighted the disconnect between financial asset performance and economic fundamentals, noting that "as long as the equity market party continues, trust in the credit-financed growth model seems intact." The 2019 introduction also examined the relationship between "financial assets of households / disposable personal income," underscoring how monetary expansion had inflated asset prices beyond sustainable levels while maintaining surface-level stability.
