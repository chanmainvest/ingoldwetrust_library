# Everything Bubble

The "Everything Bubble" is a concept describing the pervasive overvaluation of financial assets across all major classes—stocks, bonds, and real estate—driven by unprecedented monetary stimulus and money supply inflation. This phenomenon challenges traditional investment wisdom and raises fundamental questions about the sustainability of current market valuations. The term has gained prominence in investment discourse as a warning signal about systemic risks embedded in the global financial architecture.

## How the argument evolved

**2017** marked the initial articulation of this concern in the report's introduction, where the authors explicitly warned that "stocks, bonds and real estate have all become as overvalued as we have ever seen." The text positioned this as a critical juncture requiring reevaluation of the "classic investment portfolio," arguing that such widespread overvaluation across asset classes was unprecedented in modern history. The report introduced the "Everything Bubble" as a framework for understanding how monetary policy had created synchronized distortions across virtually every tradable asset.

**2019** revisited and refined this argument, adopting Jesse Felder's term "The Everything Bubble" to describe the current market environment. The analysis emphasized how excessive monetary stimulus had fostered misplaced trust in the credit-financed growth model, noting that "as long as the equity market party continues, trust in the credit-financed growth model seems intact." The 2019 introduction contextualized the bubble within household financial dynamics, particularly highlighting the ratio of "financial assets of households / disposable personal income" as a key metric revealing the unsustainable nature of current valuations. While the core premise remained consistent, the 2019 treatment placed greater emphasis on the behavioral and psychological components of the bubble—the way market participants' confidence had become intertwined with perpetually rising asset prices.
