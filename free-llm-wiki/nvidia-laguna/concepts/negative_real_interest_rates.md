# Negative Real Interest Rates

Negative real interest rates occur when nominal interest rates fall below the rate of inflation, resulting in a negative return on cash and other fixed-income assets. This economic phenomenon has become a critical lens through which investment strategists analyze the appeal of gold as both a hedge against inflation and a store of value. In the context of the *In Gold We Trust* reports, negative real interest rates are consistently presented as a catalyst for higher gold prices, reflecting a broader narrative about monetary policy, investor behavior, and the search for yield in an era of unconventional central bank interventions.

## How the argument evolved

**2008** — The report first articulates the connection between negative real interest rates and gold’s upward trajectory, identifying the United States, China, and other major economies as key regions where this dynamic is unfolding. It notes that “US real interest rates [have moved] onto negative ground, which should form a base for further increases of the gold price,” framing this condition as a structural shift rather than a temporary anomaly. The emphasis is on the macroeconomic implications: when holding cash yields less than the cost of inflation, investors are incentivized to seek alternative stores of value, with gold positioned as a natural beneficiary.

**2012** — The analysis deepens, shifting from a focus on price mechanics to investor psychology. The report observes that “negative real interest rates” create a forced risk-taking environment, where “one has to take risk to preserve one's capital.” This reframing underscores a more behavioral interpretation: in a low- or negative-yield environment, investors are pushed out of safe, low-return assets and into riskier investments—including gold. The 2012 introduction thus builds on the earlier argument by highlighting the human element: when traditional savings no longer preserve purchasing power, the allure of gold as both a speculative and defensive asset grows.

Together, these two chapters trace an evolving understanding of negative real interest rates—from a macroeconomic signal of monetary policy extremes to a behavioral driver of capital allocation decisions.
