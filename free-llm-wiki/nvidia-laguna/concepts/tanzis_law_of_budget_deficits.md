# Tanzi's Law of Budget Deficits

Tanzi's Law of Budget Deficits describes the phenomenon where rising inflation rates cause government tax revenues to grow more slowly than the overall price level, thereby increasing real budget deficits. This occurs because nominal tax collections fail to keep pace with inflation, eroding the real value of public revenues while expenditures rise in nominal terms. The law highlights a critical fiscal vulnerability during periods of high inflation, where governments face an automatic worsening of their fiscal positions even without changes in spending or tax policy.

## How the argument evolved

**2012** introduced the foundational mechanism behind Tanzi's Law, emphasizing how inflation disrupts the relationship between tax revenues and government spending. The report explained that "real budget deficits increase at a faster rate" when prices rise, primarily because "tax revenues and other public revenues do not increase in line with the inflation rate." This mismatch becomes especially problematic when the "real value of this tax declines substantially in cases of high inflation," creating a structural pressure on government finances that is independent of political decisions or spending choices.

**2020** revisited and reinforced this concept within the context of contemporary inflation dynamics, explicitly naming the principle as "Tanzi's law." The report clarified that "if inflation rates rise, tax revenues grow more slowly than the inflation rate," which directly leads to the conclusion that "which is why the budget deficit gradually increases." This evolution in the report's treatment shows a progression from describing the general fiscal consequences of inflation to identifying and naming the specific law that governs this relationship, underscoring its enduring relevance in understanding fiscal policy challenges during inflationary periods.
