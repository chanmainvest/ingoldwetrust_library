# Tanzi's Law of Budget Deficits

Tanzi's Law of Budget Deficits describes the phenomenon where rising inflation rates cause government tax revenues to grow more slowly than the overall price level, thereby increasing real budget deficits. This occurs because nominal tax collections fail to keep pace with inflation, eroding the real value of public revenues while expenditures rise in nominal terms. The law highlights a critical fiscal vulnerability during periods of high inflation, where governments face an automatic worsening of their fiscal positions even without changes in spending or tax policy.

## How the argument evolved

**2012** introduced the foundational mechanism behind Tanzi's Law, emphasizing how inflation disrupts the relationship between tax revenues and government spending. The report explained that "real budget deficits increase at a faster rate" when prices rise, because "tax revenues and other public revenues do not increase in line with the inflation rate." This mismatch meant that even if nominal revenues appeared stable, the "real value of this tax declines substantially in cases of high inflation," creating an underlying pressure on fiscal balances that was often overlooked in conventional economic analysis.

**2020** revisited and reinforced this principle within the context of contemporary inflation dynamics, explicitly naming the phenomenon as "Tanzi's law." The report clarified that "if inflation rates rise, tax revenues grow more slowly than the inflation rate," which directly leads to the conclusion that "which is why the budget deficit gradually increases." This evolution in framing showed how the core insight from 2012 had become a recognized principle in understanding modern fiscal challenges, particularly as policymakers grappled with unprecedented monetary stimulus and supply chain disruptions.

The progression from 2012 to 2020 reflects a growing recognition that Tanzi's Law is not merely a theoretical curiosity but a practical constraint on fiscal policy during inflationary periods. While the 2012 analysis focused on the mechanical relationship between prices and revenues, the 2020 report embedded this insight into a broader discussion of how inflation dynamics challenge traditional fiscal management, suggesting that the law remains as relevant today as it was nearly a decade earlier.
