# Asset Price Inflation

Asset price inflation refers to the sustained increase in the prices of assets such as real estate, stocks, and bonds, distinct from the more commonly tracked consumer price inflation. This phenomenon has become a critical focus in understanding modern monetary policy, as central banks' aggressive money supply expansion during and after the 2008 financial crisis has disproportionately fueled gains in financial and real assets rather than everyday consumer goods. The divergence between money supply growth and subdued consumer price inflation has significant implications for wealth distribution, financial stability, and the effectiveness of monetary policy transmission mechanisms.

## How the argument evolved

**2016** marked a pivotal moment in articulating the concept, with the report emphasizing how "the difference between strong money supply growth and relatively low consumer price inflation has primarily become manifest in the form of asset price inflation." The analysis highlighted that this dynamic particularly affected real estate and bonds, noting that "asset price inflation amounted to 7.8% last year, the highest level since the statistics were first compiled." This framing established asset price inflation as a distinct and measurable consequence of unconventional monetary policy, separate from traditional inflation metrics.

By **2018**, the discussion had evolved to address the broader implications of this phenomenon. The report argued that "monetary inflation does not only affect consumer prices but also asset prices," underscoring how asset prices are underrepresented in public debate despite their growing significance. It further observed that "rising share prices tend to be seen as positive," revealing a cognitive bias in how markets interpret inflationary pressures. This evolution in thinking reflected a growing recognition that the financial system's response to monetary expansion—manifested through inflated asset valuations—had become a central feature of the post-crisis economic landscape, with consequences for both savers and investors.
