# Monetary vs Price Inflation

The In Gold We Trust reports treat monetary inflation and price inflation not as interchangeable labels but as cause and effect, a distinction the authors present as essential for understanding inflation dynamics. By separating the growth of the money supply—what they call "monetary inflation"—from the subsequent "price inflation" of continuously rising prices, the corpus frames inflation as a sequential, structural process rather than a single observable symptom. This split also carries architectural implications: the texts assert that in a monetary system with a fixed or constant supply, the "general level of prices” cannot permanently increase, binding price-level behavior directly to the underlying monetary regime.

## How the argument evolved

**2016** — The chapter "15 Misunderstood Inflation" introduces the dichotomy as a corrective to widespread confusion. The report draws a sharp "difference between monetary inflation and price inflation," declaring that "Monetary inflation is the cause, price inflation is its consequence." This causal ordering is not merely semantic; it is used to show that price movements are downstream effects. The 2016 text drives the point home with a structural claim: the "general level of prices” cannot permanently increase in a monetary system with a fixed money supply." At this stage, the concept functions mainly as an educational tool, disentangling the mechanism (money creation) from its manifestation (higher prices) and grounding the discussion in the properties of the monetary base.

**2018** — In "08 Inflation Vs Deflation The Big Showdown," the same framework returns, now embedded in a broader macroeconomic confrontation. The report reminds readers that "inflation originally means 'monetary inflation'" and that "price inflation (i.e., continuously rising prices) is its consequence," preserving the 2016 causal hierarchy but sharpening the definitional vocabulary. Crucially, the 2018 chapter extends the logic to deflationary conditions, noting that price deflation can occur "In a monetary system with a (relatively) constant money supply." Where the 2016 discussion used the split primarily to correct misconceptions about rising prices, the 2018 treatment deploys it as an analytical lens for the "big showdown" between inflation and deflation. By holding the monetary definition constant, the report can contrast regimes: monetary expansion produces price inflation, while a relatively constant supply allows for price deflation. Over these two chapters, the concept thus evolves from a definitional clarification into a structural comparator of monetary systems, linking every price-level outcome back to its monetary origin.
