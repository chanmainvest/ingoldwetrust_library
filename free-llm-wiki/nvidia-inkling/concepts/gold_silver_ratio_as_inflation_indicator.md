# Gold-Silver Ratio as Inflation Indicator

The gold-silver ratio operates as a composite macro-financial gauge that captures the tension between inflationary and deflationary forces through the relative pricing of monetary metals. Rather than tracking gold or silver in isolation, the ratio distills their interaction into a single readable signal—one that the *In Gold We Trust* reports have treated both as an inflation-deflation barometer and, more recently, as a direct precursor to gold bull markets. Its significance lies in this dual role: it connects broad inflation dynamics, including derivative-market pricing, to tangible shifts in precious-metal sentiment, giving investors a cross-asset warning system that evolves with macro conditions.

## How the argument evolved

**2015** marked the report’s initial framing of the metric as an "excellent indicator of the interaction between inflation and deflation." At that stage, the emphasis was diagnostic rather than predictive: a rising gold-silver ratio was read as a disinflationary warning, with the text noting that "A rising G/S ratio is thus a warning signal for gold investors." The analysis also embedded a long-horizon structural view, reminding readers that "reversion to the mean has to take place over the long term," which positioned the ratio not as a short-term trading trigger but as a cyclical compass whose extremes eventually correct. In this early treatment, the ratio served primarily to flag the underlying health of the inflation-deflation balance, alerting gold holders that widening silver weakness relative to gold often preceded or accompanied deflationary stress.

By **2020**, the argument had sharpened considerably, integrating market-derived inflation expectations directly into the framework. The report now examined "how inflation swaps are correlated with the Gold/Silver ratio," linking the metal spread to institutional pricing of future inflation rather than treating it as a purely physical-market phenomenon. The directional interpretation also flipped in practical importance: where a rising ratio had warned of disinflation, a falling ratio became the bullish catalyst, because "A falling G/S ratio significantly increases the probability of a bull market in gold." The narrative gained concrete validation through a documented reversal—"reversal from 125 to 100 took place, which confirms our bullish outlook"—transforming the ratio from an abstract macro indicator into an actionable confirmation tool. Together, these chapters trace an evolution from theoretical interaction gauge to empirically anchored signal, one that now bridges inflation-swap markets and precious-metal bull-market probabilities within a single analytical frame.
