# Austrian Business Cycle Theory

Austrian Business Cycle Theory (ABCT) explains how artificial credit expansion causes capital goods prices to inflate ahead of consumer prices, ultimately fostering bubbles and misallocated investment. In the *In Gold We Trust* reports, the theory serves as a framework for interpreting both the sequential arrival of inflation and the monetary excesses that precede systemic crises. Its significance lies in distinguishing "asset price inflation" from "consumer price inflation"—a temporal split that allows analysts to detect monetary distortion in capital markets long before it reaches headline consumer indices.

## How the argument evolved

**2015** — The report introduces ABCT as an immediate interpretive lens for contemporary market behavior. The chapter states that "Current developments correspond precisely to the textbook Austrian Business Cycle Theory (ABCT)," framing observed price dynamics not as random fluctuations but as the predictable first stage of a monetary cycle. The mechanism is clearly delineated: "prices of capital goods (=asset price inflation) thus rise first," creating visible appreciation in assets and capital markets, while "consumer prices begin to rise (=consumer price inflation) only later." By highlighting this lag, the 2015 treatment establishes ABCT as a chronological map—warning observers not to confuse early capital-market exuberance with absent inflation, but rather to recognize it as the leading edge of broader price distortion.

**2016** — Building on that sequential understanding, the report turns to root-cause analysis, applying ABCT retrospectively to the pre-2008 era. The notes identify the "disproportionate growth rate of M3 in the time period before 2008 was the root cause" of the subsequent crisis, describing the episode as a "textbook example of Austrian business cycle theory (ABCT)." Rather than treating the bubble as an isolated market failure, the 2016 analysis shows how excessive monetary growth can "promote malinvestment, distort the economic structures," channeling resources into capital goods and speculative assets long before consumer prices reflect the underlying expansion. Read together, the two chapters form a complete arc: **2015** demonstrates the theory's descriptive power in tracking inflation's staged arrival, while **2016** confirms its explanatory power by linking that same staged pattern back to measurable monetary overgrowth and its destructive structural consequences.
