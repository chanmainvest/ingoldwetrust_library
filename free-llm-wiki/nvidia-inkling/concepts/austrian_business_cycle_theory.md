# Austrian Business Cycle Theory

Austrian Business Cycle Theory (ABCT) holds that artificial credit expansion drives interest rates below their natural level, distorting the structure of production and funneling investment into capital goods that do not align with actual consumer preferences. Within the In Gold We Trust reports, the theory functions as both a diagnostic framework for inflation sequencing and an explanatory model for crisis dynamics, emphasizing that monetary distortion produces asset-price inflation long before it registers in consumer prices. Its significance in the corpus is therefore twofold: it distinguishes capital-goods inflation from consumer-price inflation, and it traces systemic bubbles back to disproportionate monetary growth rather than to market failure alone.

## How the argument evolved

**2015** introduces ABCT as a precise description of then-current price behavior. Rather than treating inflation as a uniform phenomenon, the report observes that "Current developments correspond precisely to the textbook Austrian Business Cycle Theory (ABCT)." The narrative highlights a temporal split: "prices of capital goods (=asset price inflation) thus rise first," creating visible inflation in assets, while "consumer prices begin to rise (=consumer price inflation) only later." This sequencing allows the report to interpret rising asset valuations not as isolated market exuberance but as the predictable first stage of a credit-induced cycle, with consumer-price effects lagging behind.

By **2016**, the argument deepens from descriptive sequencing to causal attribution, applying the framework retrospectively to the pre-2008 period. The report argues that the "disproportionate growth rate of M3 in the time period before 2008 was the root cause" of the capital misallocation that preceded the crisis, labeling the episode a "textbook example of Austrian business cycle theory (ABCT)." In this reading, excessive monetary expansion did not merely precede the bubble; it actively served to "promote malinvestment, distort the economic structures," and generate the systemic fragility that eventually collapsed. The evolution is clear: where the 2015 chapter uses ABCT to decode an ongoing inflation asymmetry, the 2016 chapter deploys it as a forensic tool, identifying rapid M3 growth as the mechanism that translated loose monetary policy into widespread economic distortion.

Together, these chapters construct a continuous narrative in which ABCT bridges micro-level price dynamics and macro-level crisis explanation. The theory moves from explaining why capital goods inflate ahead of consumer goods to demonstrating how unchecked monetary aggregates can engineer the very bubbles that necessitate painful correction.
