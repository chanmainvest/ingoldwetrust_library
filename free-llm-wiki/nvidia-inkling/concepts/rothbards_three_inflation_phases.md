# Rothbard's Three Inflation Phases

Rothbard's three inflation phases describe a progressive monetary deterioration in which an expanding money supply initially suppresses visible prices, then gradually reshapes public expectations, and ultimately culminates in a terminal flight from currency. The framework is significant because it demonstrates that inflation is not a linear or uniform process but a staged collapse—moving from hidden monetary growth through anticipatory behavior to either currency abandonment or hyperinflation—thereby providing a structural warning for investors tracking gold and monetary dynamics.

## How the argument evolved

**2015** first brought the framework into the report's analysis of gold and inflation, presenting Rothbard's outline of three phases that end in currency abandonment and the crack-up boom. In this early treatment, the report emphasized "Rothbard defines the desperate fight against deflation" as the conceptual backdrop, noting that "In the first phase, the money supply is increased, but prices barely rise"—a period where monetary expansion remains largely invisible to the public. The narrative then pointed toward the terminal stage, observing that "Phase three is commonly called the “crack-up boom”", framing the endpoint as an outright abandonment of the currency.

By **2020**, the report revisited the same Rothbardian sequence within its examination of inflation dynamics, sharpening the endpoint from mere abandonment to explicit hyperinflation and underscoring the psychological mechanism driving the collapse. The first phase was restated with slight precision: "In the first phase the money supply is increased, but prices hardly rise at all." The second phase received fuller attention, with the report noting that "In the second phase, the public begins to anticipate slowly rising prices"—a critical shift in expectation that accelerates velocity. Finally, the terminal stage was broadened: "Phase 3 is commonly referred to as the “crack-up boom” or “hyperinflation”", explicitly linking the crack-up boom to hyperinflation and highlighting that public expectation shifts until demand for money collapses. Together, these treatments show the report maintaining the core three-phase architecture while deepening its emphasis on expectation dynamics and clarifying the final stage's hyperinflationary nature.
