# Gold Seasonality Pattern

The gold seasonality pattern describes the recurring annual cycle in which gold prices tend to strengthen during specific months and stagnate during others, driven by cultural demand surges, holiday stocking, and structural market rhythms. Across the In Gold We Trust reports, this pattern is treated not as a statistical curiosity but as a practical framework for timing entries and exits, with successive editions refining both the calendar window and the magnitude of the effect. Its significance rests on the extreme concentration of returns: rather than being spread evenly across the year, "the vast bulk of gold's 7.5% average annual price gain" is compressed into a discrete seasonal window, while long intervening periods produce negligible movement.

## How the argument evolved

**2012** laid the cultural groundwork. The report tied price strength to identifiable global consumption rituals, noting that "The so-called wedding season and the Diwali festival in India result in a seasonal uptick in demand," while Christmas stocking further bolstered the market. This produced a clear Q4 and Q1 bias, with the authors observing that "gold tends to perform best in the fourth and the first quarter" and that "This seasonality has been observed in 75-80% of the cases." At this stage, the argument was largely qualitative, linking price strength to seasonal demand without precise quantification.

By **2014**, the analysis had sharpened into a tactical calendar. The report declared that "Gold displays pronounced seasonality," identifying June as the favorable entry point—"June is traditionally a good time to buy"—and September as the apex, since "September is traditionally the strongest month." The second half of the year was singled out as the period when "In the second half of the year upward momentum is clearly at its highest," shifting the narrative from cultural explanation toward actionable seasonal positioning.

**2015** reinforced and codified these findings. The authors noted that "We have already discussed the fact that gold exhibits clear seasonal trends," reiterating that "Traditionally, June is an excellent time for purchases" and confirming that "Seasonal upside momentum is clearly at its strongest in the second half of the year." The repetition across editions signaled growing confidence in the pattern, even as the framing remained descriptive rather than precisely measured.

The treatment turned quantitative in **2017**. Rather than speaking in quarters or months alone, the report defined a precise window: "gold prices typically rise from early August to the end of February," a span that generates "the vast bulk of gold's 7.5% average annual price gain." The remaining portion of the year was shown to be essentially flat; "the seasonal return generated in the remaining 167 days from 21 February until August 6 amounted to a negligible 0.30%." This marked a decisive evolution—from observing that gold exhibits "clear seasonal trends" to demonstrating that nearly all annual performance is confined to roughly half the calendar.

**2018** both widened and contextualized the window. The report stated that "Gold typically rises from the beginning of July to the end of February," emphasizing that "Almost the entire average annual gain tends to occur during that period." Crucially, the analysis added a political dimension: "In years of US midterm elections, which tend to be very positive years as far as the performance of gold is concerned," the seasonal effect appears amplified. Thus, the argument matured from cultural seasonality to a quantified, politically aware cyclical model.
