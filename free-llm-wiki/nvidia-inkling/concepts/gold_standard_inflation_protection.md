# Gold Standard Inflation Protection

The concept of Gold Standard Inflation Protection treats the gold-backed monetary regime as the most reliable historical defense against currency debasement and rising prices. Rather than framing gold merely as a speculative hedge, the *In Gold We Trust* reports present the standard itself as an institutional mechanism that enforced price stability through disciplined supply constraints. Its significance rests on a stark empirical contrast: the gold era delivered minimal inflation and exceptionally low volatility, while the fiat regimes that followed—particularly after the collapse of Bretton Woods—exhibited both higher average price increases and greater instability.

## How the argument evolved

**2009** — In "20 Is Gold Pricing In The Coming Inflation," the report asks whether gold markets are already discounting an impending inflationary surge. Within that framing, it issues a clear historical judgment: "gold standard is proven to offer the best protection against inflation". The quantitative record is unambiguous—"annual inflation from 1879 to 1914 was 0.2% at a volatility of only 2.2%"—demonstrating that the classical gold standard produced not merely low prices, but remarkably predictable ones. The report then measures this stability against the modern fiat experience, observing that "Since 1971, volatility has been 2.8%, with average inflation at 4.6%", thereby linking the abandonment of gold to a simultaneous rise in both price-level growth and market turbulence.

**2010** — The analysis in "03 Inflation Vs Deflation" builds on this foundation by explicitly contrasting the gold standard with the post-1971 Bretton Woods era of fiat currency. The central claim is reiterated with formal emphasis: "The gold standard is proven to offer the best protection against inflation". Once again, the historical benchmark is precise—"annual price inflation from 1879 to 1914 was 0.2% at a volatility of only 2.2%"—while the contemporary comparison is anchored to the end of the gold-exchange system: "Since 1971 (i.e. the end of Bretton Woods), volatility has been 2.8%". By characterizing the divergence as a direct regime comparison—gold-backed discipline versus fiat flexibility—the 2010 chapter transforms the prior year’s observation into a structured thesis: the gold standard delivered far lower inflation and volatility than the monetary architecture that replaced it, making it the definitive benchmark for inflation protection.
