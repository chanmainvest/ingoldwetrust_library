# Negative Money Supply Growth

Negative money supply growth describes a contraction in broad monetary aggregates—specifically the US M2 measure—where the total stock of money and credit in the economy shrinks rather than expands. In the 2023 *In Gold We Trust* reports, this phenomenon is treated not as a routine cyclical adjustment but as a historically exceptional rupture with direct recessionary implications. The reports frame the contraction as "a sure sign of profound economic dislocation," linking shrinking liquidity to collapsing credit demand, disinflationary pressure, and deteriorating real-time economic indicators.

## How the argument evolved

**2023** The report first establishes the historical magnitude of the contraction in its opening chapter, noting that US M2 "is negative for the first time since the 1950s" and, even more starkly, "and on an annual basis for the first time since the Great Depression." By placing the data in this long historical context, the authors present negative money supply growth as a generational break rather than a temporary fluctuation. They immediately underscore its functional meaning: "A declining money and credit supply is a sure sign of profound economic dislocation," signaling that the contraction should be read as a direct precursor to recession rather than a benign statistical outlier.

**2023** As the analysis moves to the status quo of inflation, the same monetary fact is revisited with deeper macroeconomic interpretation. The chapter confirms that "In the USA, money supply growth is negative for the first time since the Great Depression," but now integrates it with collapsing credit demand and broader recessionary disinflationary signals. The narrative insists that "Recessions are always disinflationary," using the shrinking M2 to counter any lingering inflationary expectations and to reinforce the case for an impending economic slump. The authors further anchor the monetary data in observable conditions, observing that "The inverted yield curve and poor consumer sentiment are already strong signs of an economic slump." Across these successive treatments, the 2023 report evolves from documenting an unprecedented monetary contraction to interpreting it as the leading edge of a disinflationary recession driven by vanishing credit and weakening sentiment.
