# Global De-Dollarization Trend

The global de-dollarization trend describes the accelerating retreat from US dollar usage in international trade, central-bank reserves, and energy transactions, a shift that directly threatens Washington’s reserve-currency dominance. Rather than a temporary market fluctuation, the report treats this as a structural endgame in which “The US Dollar Storyline is coming to an end” and the “dollar’s reserve currency status will continue to give ground.” Its significance lies in the strategic realignment of major economies—particularly through digital-currency initiatives and energy partnerships—that collectively erode the institutional foundations of dollar hegemony.

## How the argument evolved

**2020** — The report frames de-dollarization as a broad, systemic withdrawal rather than a localized policy shift. It observes that “Many countries in the world began turning away from using the dollar,” reducing its role not only in trade but also in reserves and energy markets. The tone is conclusive: “The US Dollar Storyline is coming to an end,” and the analysis warns that the “dollar’s reserve currency status will continue to give ground.” At this stage, the argument emphasizes diffusion—many actors across multiple sectors—without identifying a single strategic architect. The significance is cumulative: reserve-currency dominance is eroding through accumulated national choices rather than a single decisive break.

**2021** — The narrative sharpens from diffuse trend to targeted state strategy. China moves to the center as an active challenger, and the report records that “it is a stated goal of China to use the e-yuan to slowly undermine the dominance of the US dollar.” This digital-currency initiative is explicitly designed to “challenge the dominance of the US dollar in international trade settlement,” adding a technological front to the trade and reserve dynamics outlined a year earlier. The geopolitical scope widens further: “Fifty years after the Nixon shock, even Saudi Arabia is openly turning toward China,” signaling that energy exporters—long the bedrock of dollar demand—are now openly realigning. Together, the two chapters trace an evolution from recognizing widespread dollar retreat to mapping the specific mechanisms—state-backed digital currencies and energy-diplomacy pivots—accelerating it.
