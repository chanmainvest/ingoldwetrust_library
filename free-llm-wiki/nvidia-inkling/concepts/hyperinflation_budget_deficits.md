# Hyperinflation Budget Deficits

Hyperinflation budget deficits describe the catastrophic monetary collapse that follows when governments finance enormous fiscal shortfalls through discretionary paper-currency creation. The In Gold We Trust reports treat this mechanism as historically universal, linking massive state spending gaps—specifically deficits reaching critical shares of public outlays—to the inevitable destruction of monetary value. Recognizing this relationship is essential to understanding why fiat regimes, rather than commodity-backed standards, provide both the tool and the motive for runaway price increases.

## How the argument evolved

The report’s analysis begins with a historical law and matures into a present-day fiscal alarm. **2012** establishes the foundational proof in "Inflation Rising Prices Confusing Terminology With Grave Consequences," where Bernholz demonstrates that hyperinflation is never accidental but always the product of fiscal profligacy under fiat money. The chapter insists that "every hyperinflation, ever, has happened under a discretionary paper currency standard," leaving no ambiguity about the monetary precondition for collapse. Bernholz removes any doubt about causation, stating there is "no example in history where hyperinflation was not caused by money creation due to exorbitant budget deficits." By tying currency destruction directly to state financing needs, the analysis shows that discretionary paper money serves as the enabling mechanism, while the deficit supplies the fuel. The report also introduces a quantitative boundary, warning that "Budget deficits of 40% in terms of 40% of public spending will eventually result in hyperinflation," framing the 40 percent share of government outlays as the critical tipping point beyond which stability collapses.

**2020** revisits this framework in "Status Quo Of Inflation Dynamics" and confirms that the historical pattern remains intact. Bernholz shows that hyperinflation still follows discretionary paper money and large budget deficits, reiterating that "all hyperinflations have occurred in the wake of discretionary paper money standards" and that "budget deficits amounting to 40% of government spending are a sure path to hyperinflation." The mechanism and threshold are unchanged, but the application is sharpened: the report no longer treats hyperinflation as a distant historical curiosity. Instead, it warns that "Current forecasts for the US are already well above the higher figure," signaling that modern American fiscal projections have surpassed the critical deficit thresholds defined in the earlier analysis. Where the 2012 chapter established the rule and measured the boundary, the 2020 chapter verifies its persistence and declares its immediate relevance, transforming an historical observation into an active policy warning.
